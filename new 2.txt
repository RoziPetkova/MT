Gaze × Experiment in the combined LMM: F(2,22458)=0.22, p=.804 (n.s.). Same conclusion in the plain mixed ANOVA on raw RT: F(2,135)=0.06, p=.939 (n.s.).

So the basic gaze-cueing effect itself — how strongly gaze direction speeds up target detection, independent of what action the participant did — is the same size whether the response was a keypress, a mouse click, or a touchscreen tap. Response modality doesn't touch the core reflexive-orienting phenomenon at all; it replicates cleanly across all three.

What response modality does touch is the interaction — how much the action-consistency manipulation (did your button press match, mismatch, or have nothing to do with the gaze shift) modulates that gaze-cueing effect. That's the Gaze × Action × Experiment term, marginal on raw RT (p=.061) and significant on log-RT/Cohen's d (p=.018/.013) — and it's specifically located in the inconsistent condition, Exp1 (keypress) vs Exp3 (touch).

So the story splits cleanly in two: the gaze-cueing effect is a stable, modality-independent phenomenon; whether your own action feeds back into how strong that effect is depends on how physically embodied the action is. That's a fairly clean theoretical distinction to have data support — the basic attentional mechanism doesn't care how you responded, but the self-monitoring/agency piece does.

Simple effects (what "GC effect by condition" is): used specifically to decompose an interaction. The question is "does Factor A's effect hold up within this one level of Factor B?" — you hold one factor fixed and test the other. This is the standard, expected follow-up when a 2-way interaction is significant (Exp2/Exp3 here) — you don't just report "the interaction is significant" and stop, you show where it lives. It's also legitimate (as exploratory, clearly labeled) to run even when the interaction isn't significant, as a descriptive check — which is what we did for Exp1.

Post-hoc tests: used to decompose a significant main effect that has 3+ levels, when you don't yet know which levels differ from which. E.g., if the Action main effect (3 levels: consistent/control/inconsistent) is significant, a post-hoc test (Holm, Tukey, Bonferroni) tells you specifically whether consistent differs from control, control from inconsistent, and consistent from inconsistent. This is about pinning down pairwise differences within one factor, not about how two factors combine.

One-way ANOVA: used when there's genuinely only one factor to test — either because the whole design only has one factor, or because you've deliberately restricted/sliced the data to isolate one specific comparison. We used this differently elsewhere: e.g., testing whether the size of the GC effect (already collapsed into one number per subject) differs by Experiment — that's a one-way ANOVA because at that point Experiment is the only factor left; the within-subject structure has already been reduced to a single per-subject value beforehand.

--------------------------------
"Collapsed" means averaged across the levels of every other factor — comparing groups on just one factor while ignoring how the others vary.

Concretely: "Action, collapsed" (Mixed ANOVA) means the consistent/inconsistent/control comparison uses each subject's overall mean RT in that condition, averaged over both congruent and incongruent trials and over all three experiments — it doesn't care whether a given trial was gaze-congruent or which experiment the subject was in, just "what was their average RT when in the consistent condition" vs. "when in inconsistent" vs. "when in control."

Contrast that with "Experiment×Gaze×Action" (not collapsed on anything) — there, every comparison is between one specific cell (e.g., "Experiment2, incongruent, consistent") and another specific cell, nothing averaged away.

And "Experiment, collapsed" (Cohen's d / GC-effect) means comparing Exp1 vs. Exp2 vs. Exp3 using each subject's overall d/effect-size averaged across all three action conditions — ignoring which specific condition (consistent/inconsistent/control) that number came from.

So it's really a dial: fully collapsed = broadest, most averaged-over comparison (most statistical power, least specific); fully uncollapsed = most granular, cell-by-cell comparison (least power once corrected, most specific about exactly where an effect lives). The reason both versions matter here: "Action, collapsed" told us active-vs-control is robust in general; "Experiment×Gaze×Action" (uncollapsed) told us specifically that the reversal lives in Exp2/Exp3's inconsistent condition — the collapsed version would have averaged that specificity away and hidden it.

                                                                                                               What it actually means

d_incons is your implicit measure — how much a person's attention still gets captured by the face's gaze       specifically in trials where their own cdirection pointed opposite ways.inconsistent_composite_ipsat is their explicit rating in that same condition, relative to their own personal baseline — positive means "I still felt more in control here than my own average," negative means "I felt less in control here than my own average" (i.tch).

The correlation is negative: people who kept feeling relatively agentic despite the mismatch show a more       reversed gaze-cueing effect (attention pose, away from where the face looked);people whose felt agency dropped in that condition — who noticed something went wrong — show a more normal,    gaze-following attentional pattern.

In plain terms: when someone's conscious sense of "that was my doing" fails to update in response to an actual action-outcome mismatch, that same miscaly as their attention sticking to theirown intended direction instead of following the external social cue. When the conscious system does correctly register the error, attention reverts to the normal, gaze-following default. It's a link between explicit error-monitoring and automatic/reflexivek that only exists when there's an actual error to monitor, which is exactly why it's silent in consistent (nothing violated) and control (no intention to violate).

It also lines up with the modality gradient already running through your whole RT chapter — the link is weakest in Exp1 (keypress), stronger in Exp2 (mouse), strongest in Exp3 (touchscreen). The more spatially concrete and effortful the action, the t coupling gets specifically around themoment it's violated.
