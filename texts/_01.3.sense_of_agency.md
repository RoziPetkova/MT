## Part III — Sense of Agency

### 1. Introduction: From Free Will to Sense of Agency

The problem of free will has run through the history of Western thought like a red thread: are we, as individuals, the authors of our actions, or are they determined by external causes? Psychology does not aim to solve this philosophical problem, but it investigates a related concept that is far more empirically accessible: the sense of agency (SoA). This is defined as the subjective experience of being the initiator and controller of one's own actions and of their effects on the environment (Gallagher, 2000; Haggard & Tsakiris, 2009; Haggard, 2017). Three fundamental aspects underpin SoA: intention (the desired state that one pursues through action), action (awareness of one's own actions), and effect (perception of the consequences of action) (Wen & Imamizu, 2022). These three components are directly linked to the motor, perceptual, and sensorimotor systems, each playing a major role in generating SoA.

Before turning to the theories of SoA, it is important to emphasize from the outset that SoA does not refer to a global attitude towards life, to one's locus of control, or to a general sense of being responsible for one's life, but to specific experiences tied to isolated events or particular action sequences (Dewey & Knoblich, 2014; Pacherie, 2008). This is the sense in which SoA is studied in cognitive science. Accordingly, in the laboratory, SoA is studied through such micro-events, typically by introducing a causal relationship between the participant's action and a sensory effect, in which the experimenter distorts the temporal, spatial, or outcome relationship between the two (Farrer et al., 2003; Metcalfe & Greene, 2007; Sato & Yasuda, 2005). This type of design allows for fine manipulation of SoA and its systematic measurement, and the present work builds on the same logic.

### 2. Distinguishing SoA from the Sense of Ownership

An important distinction must be made between SoA and the sense of ownership (SoO). Whereas SoA is the experience of initiating and controlling an action (Braun et al., 2018), SoO refers to the awareness that one's own body and its parts belong to oneself (Braun et al., 2018; see also Gallagher, 2000; Tsakiris et al., 2007). Although the two are closely related, they can be dissociated: I can feel that my leg belongs to me (ownership) without feeling that I initiated its movement (absence of agency), which is the difference between "I moved my arm" and "my arm was moved". Such dissociations occur both in everyday life, for example in the case of tics or involuntary movements, and in clinical conditions such as Tourette syndrome or anarchic hand syndrome (Braun et al., 2018; Zapparoli et al., 2020). In the laboratory, this distinction has been demonstrated through the rubber hand illusion, in which participants can feel that the fake hand belongs to them (SoO) without feeling that they control it (SoA) (Braun et al., 2018; Tsakiris et al., 2007). The two experiences thus rest on different processes: SoA depends on the match between the motor intention and the sensory feedback of the action, whereas SoO can arise from the match between what is seen and what is felt, even in the absence of any voluntary movement (Tsakiris et al., 2007). Accordingly, when the predicted consequences of an action are disrupted, SoA is reduced while SoO remains intact (Sato & Yasuda, 2005).

The opposite dissociation is also possible: SoA can exist without ownership, extending beyond one's own body (Haggard, 2017). For example, a person controlling an avatar in a video game feels a sense of agency over it because the avatar's movements correspond to the buttons they press, even though its detailed movements are generated by the computer. If a computer error suddenly breaks this correspondence, the person may temporarily lose SoA over the avatar (Wen & Imamizu, 2022).

The essence of SoA, therefore, lies not in the embodied experience as such, but in the fact that it allows us to monitor our own intentional actions and to distinguish them from external causes that can also make events happen (Frith, Blakemore & Wolpert, 2000; Haggard & Tsakiris, 2009). One influential proposal is that, when we act, the brain constantly compares the predicted sensory consequences of the action with the actual feedback; a mismatch generates an error signal that enables us to adjust our behaviour and reduces the sense of agency (Engbert et al., 2008; Frith, Blakemore & Wolpert, 2000). This illustrates a central principle of SoA: the match between action and effect. Moreover, because agency can extend beyond the body, it can also play a role in social interaction, allowing us to discern whether an outcome was produced by us, by our partner, or by both together (see Le Besnerais et al., 2024, for a review). How exactly SoA arises, however, is still debated, and the main theoretical accounts are introduced below.

### 3. Theoretical Models of SoA

Four major theoretical models have been proposed to explain the generation of SoA. These models are not mutually exclusive, but complementary: together they provide a comprehensive framework that spans from simple sensorimotor mechanisms to complex cognitive inferences.

#### 3.1 The Comparator Model

The comparator model is the classical account of SoA (Haggard, 2017). According to it, the motor control system uses five types of representations (Carruthers, 2010; Frith, Blakemore & Wolpert, 2000): (a) the goal state — the desired final position of the body, also called motor intention; (b) the motor commands, of which two copies are produced through the inverse model; the copy that remains central is called the corollary discharge or efference copy; (c) the predicted sensory consequences, generated by the forward model based on the efference copy; (d) the actual sensory consequences of the movement (reafference); (e) an estimation of body position, resulting from the continuous combination of predicted and actual information. SoA arises when the comparison between (c) and (d) indicates a match; when the two do not coincide, the event is perceived as external, and SoA is reduced or absent (Frith, Blakemore & Wolpert, 2000; Haggard, 2017).

However, the comparator model has significant limitations. Originally developed as a theory of motor control and only later applied to SoA, it applies mainly to simple motor actions, with immediate and predictable sensory consequences, and does not explain SoA for complex actions, with delayed effects or multiple possible consequences (Braun et al., 2018; Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014; Haggard, 2017). Moreover, it explains the implicit, low-level component of agency (feeling of agency, FoA) only partly, and fails to explain the explicit judgment of agency (JoA), which involves conceptual inferences and social context (Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008). In addition, the model requires case-by-case adjustments to explain problematic data. Contextual cues such as priming can increase SoA without any change in the match between action and outcome, which the model cannot account for (Carruthers, 2010). More generally, a match or mismatch in one sensory modality does not have a fixed effect on SoA: in some conditions, actions are still experienced as self-produced despite a clear mismatch between predicted and actual feedback, for example when hand movements are shown visually distorted or one's own voice is played back at a different pitch, whereas in others, congruent visual feedback is not enough for SoA when proprioceptive feedback is missing, as in a deafferented patient (Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008). This observation suggests that different sensory modalities and cues have different weights depending on context (Pacherie, 2013) — which led to the development of the multifactorial weighting model of Synofzik and colleagues (2008).

#### 3.2 Wegner's Retrospective Theory

The second major theoretical model — and the one that complements the comparator model — is the retrospective (or postdictive) theory proposed by Wegner (2002; Wegner & Wheatley, 1999). Unlike the comparator model, which explains SoA through the comparison of prediction with actual sensory feedback, the retrospective theory holds that SoA is not based on prediction, but on a post-hoc causal inference: agency is experienced when the brain infers that its own thoughts caused the action, based on contextual cues available after the outcome.

Wegner (2002) proposed that this causal inference relies on three principles that together determine how strongly SoA is experienced. The first is priority: the thought or intention must temporally precede the action. If the intention appears after the action, the causal link cannot be established. The second is exclusivity: there must be no other plausible causes of the action. If an external agent could have produced the action, self-attribution is reduced. The third is consistency: the observed action and its outcome must match the initial intention. If the outcome differs from what was intended, the causal inference is weakened (Wegner, 2002; Wegner & Wheatley, 1999; see also Wen & Imamizu, 2022). This logic mirrors causal inference in science: the cause must precede the effect, must be the only plausible cause, and must fit the effect (Wegner & Wheatley, 1999).

These three principles were demonstrated experimentally in Wegner's classic studies. In the "helping hands" experiment (Wegner, Sparrow & Winerman, 2004), participants stood with their arms at their sides while a confederate moved their arms underneath, so that the confederate's movements appeared to be the participant's. When participants heard instructions describing the confederate's movements, they reported stronger SoA over those movements — even though they did not execute them. In the "I-spy" experiment (Wegner & Wheatley, 1999), participants believed they were jointly controlling a cursor on a screen with a confederate; when the confederate forced the cursor to stop on an object, and the object's name had been mentioned 5 or 1 second earlier, participants reported stronger SoA over the stop — even though they had not controlled it. These studies demonstrate that SoA can be induced by contextual cues (priority, consistency), even in the absence of a real action.

However, the retrospective theory also has limitations. It does not explain how SoA arises for simple, automatised actions like involuntary postural adjustments, in which there is no explicit conscious intention nor for skilled actions performed 'in the zone' (Carruthers, 2010). A pianist playing a fast, complex solo, for example, does not consciously choose each note or fingering, yet still experiences the performance as their own. Nor does it explain why the feeling of agency is absent under conditions in which sensorimotor prediction fails but the context is favorable (e.g., in the case of passive movements in Dubynin & Shishkin, 2017).

#### 3.3 The Bayesian Integration Model

Both the comparator model and Wegner's retrospective theory partially explain SoA, but neither succeeds in integrating all experimental data. This mutual limitation led to the development of a third model — the cue integration model or Bayesian model — which proposes that SoA does not depend on a single type of cue, but on the weighted integration of multiple sources of information, both prospective and retrospective (Moore & Haggard, 2008, 2010; Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008b).

According to this model, the brain computes the probability that an event is caused by the self ($p(\text{self} \mid \text{outcome})$) based on a Bayesian rule: the probability is equal to the product of the probability that the self produced that outcome ($p(\text{outcome} \mid \text{self})$) and the prior probability that the self is the agent ($p(\text{self})$), divided by the general probability of the outcome ($p(\text{outcome})$) (Moore & Haggard, 2010; Wen & Imamizu, 2022):

$$
p(\text{self} \mid \text{outcome}) = \frac{p(\text{outcome} \mid \text{self}) \times p(\text{self})}{p(\text{outcome})}
$$

In other words, SoA is not a binary process (present or absent), but a probabilistic continuum that depends on how likely it is that one's action caused the observed effect.

A crucial aspect of this model is that the weight of each type of cue depends on context — more precisely, on its perceived reliability. When sensorimotor cues are clear and consistent (e.g., precise and real-time visual feedback), they receive a higher weight. When these cues are weak, ambiguous, or inconsistent, the weight shifts toward contextual and cognitive cues (e.g., expectations, beliefs, social context). This flexibility explains why SoA can be maintained even under conditions in which one type of cue is disrupted (Moore & Haggard, 2008, 2010; Wen & Imamizu, 2022).

#### 3.4 Predictive Processing

The fourth theoretical model — predictive processing — extends Bayesian integration to a hierarchical architecture of the brain (Friston, 2012). According to this framework, the brain is a system that continuously generates predictions about the hidden causes of sensory input and attempts to minimise prediction error — the discrepancy between prediction and actual feedback (Apps & Tsakiris, 2014; Friston, 2010, 2012). SoA, in this view, is not a separate module, but may arise from the brain's model of what caused its own movements, integrating prediction errors at multiple hierarchical levels, from low-level sensorimotor levels to high-level cognitive levels (Friston, 2012).

A central concept in predictive processing is precision weighting. Not all prediction errors are treated equally: the brain assigns each source of information a precision (the inverse of its perceived variability), which determines how much that error matters in updating predictions. When a sensory signal is clear and consistent, its precision is high, and the associated prediction error carries greater weight. When the signal is ambiguous or inconsistent, its precision is low, and the error carries less weight (Friston, 2010). Precision weighting also connects agency and attention: attending to a stimulus corresponds to increasing the precision of its prediction errors, whereas during self-generated movement the precision of its sensory consequences is attenuated, as if attention were withdrawn from them (Brown et al., 2013; Friston, 2010). 

Despite their differences, all four models converge on the same principle: SoA is strongest when the observed outcome matches the expected one. They differ, however, in which signals they consider and in when the match is evaluated, before, during or after the action. Beyond these explanatory models, SoA can also be described along several dimensions, which are outlined in the next section.

### 4. Components and Classifications of SoA

#### 4.1 The Feeling of Agency (FoA) and the Judgment of Agency (JoA)

An important distinction in the conceptual delimitation of SoA is that between the feeling of agency (FoA) and the judgment of agency (JoA) (Gallagher, 2012; Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008b; Bayne & Pacherie, 2007). According to this dual model, SoA is not a unitary phenomenon, but manifests at two distinct levels of processing.

FoA is an experience represented by an immediate, pre-reflective, and non-conceptual sensation, which does not require conscious reflection or deliberation (Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008). It arises during the action itself, from sensorimotor prediction and the incoming feedback of its effects, and is closely tied to immediate bodily signals (proprioception, efference copy) (Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008; Gallagher, 2012). In other words, FoA is the raw sensation that "I am doing this" — a sensation that is not directly accessible to introspection and that manifests through behaviour, not through reflection (Gallagher, 2012). It answers the question: "do I feel that I am doing this?"

JoA, by contrast, is a high-level process — a reflective, conceptual, and controlled judgment that arises after the outcome. It relies on an interpretation about the authorship of a particular action, constructed from comparing prediction with actual feedback and integrating contextual information (Synofzik et al., 2008a, 2008b). JoA involves concepts (causality, authorship, intention) and beliefs about the link between action and outcome. It answers the question: "do I believe that I did this?"

It is important to emphasise that the two levels occupy different positions in the hierarchy of cognitive processing, without interpreting this in terms of importance or quality of experience. FoA is defined mainly by sensory input and bodily signals, whereas JoA is closer to complex cognition, concepts, and inferences (Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008). Both levels are part of SoA, but they operate differently and can be influenced by different factors. The two aspects are not always aligned and can be empirically dissociated: in studies on passive movements, participants did not report FoA for movements executed by a mechanical device, but they did report JoA — the belief that the action could have been theirs — based on the experimental context (Dubynin & Shishkin, 2017). More generally, implicit and explicit measures of SoA can dissociate and are often not correlated with each other (Dewey & Knoblich, 2014; Moore, Middleton, Haggard & Fletcher, 2012), a point discussed further in the section on the measurement of SoA.

#### 4.2 Prospective and Retrospective Cues: Mapping onto the Experimental Design

A further classification of SoA concerns the temporal moment at which a certain type of information contributes to agency. We can distinguish between prospective cues, which appear before or during the action, and retrospective cues, which appear after the outcome (Chambon & Haggard, 2012; Haggard, 2017). This distinction is directly relevant to the design of the present study, as discussed below.

Prospective cues are signals available before or during the action, which anticipate the outcome and can determine SoA even before the effect is observed (Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014; Haggard & Chambon, 2012). These include action fluency (how easily and naturally response selection and preparation unfold), the intention itself (the mental representation of the action goal), and motor preparation (the readiness state of the motor system) (Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014; Gallagher, 2012). Chambon and Haggard (2012; see also Wenke, Fleming & Haggard, 2010) demonstrated that action fluency predicts SoA independently of actual motor performance: when response selection is fluent, participants report stronger SoA, even if the action outcome is identical. In a subsequent study, Chambon, Moore and Haggard (2015) showed that transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) over the inferior parietal cortex at the time of action selection disrupts the sense of control over the subsequent effects of the action, suggesting that this region is involved in generating the prospective signals that support SoA. In the present design, the prospective component is indexed in part by initiation time — the latency between face onset and the participant's action — which likely reflects intention formation, motor preparation, response selection fluency, and anticipation of the effect's timing.

Retrospective cues, by contrast, are signals available after the outcome, which contribute to SoA through outcome monitoring and action-effect matching (Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014; Haggard, 2017). These include comparing the initial prediction with actual sensory feedback, integrating contextual information about causality, and evaluating consequences (Haggard, 2017; Moore & Obhi, 2012). In comparator model terminology, retrospective cues correspond to detecting the match or mismatch between the efference copy and actual feedback (Frith, Blakemore & Wolpert, 2000). In Wegner's (2002; Wegner & Wheatley, 1999) terminology, they correspond to the consistency criterion — the extent to which the observed outcome matches the initial intention. In the present design, the retrospective component is captured by the action–outcome consistency (AOC) condition (consistent/inconsistent/control), which reflects the match or mismatch between the intended direction and the observed direction of the avatar's gaze.

A robust SoA does not rely exclusively on one type of cue, but integrates both sources of information, with their relative weight depending on context (Moore & Haggard, 2008, 2010; Haggard, 2017). When prospective cues are clear and fluent, they can support SoA even before the outcome appears (Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014; Moore & Haggard, 2008). When prospective cues are weak or ambiguous, SoA becomes more dependent on retrospective cues — on the match between what was intended and what was observed (Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014; Moore & Haggard, 2008). This complementarity explains why SoA can be maintained even under conditions in which one of the two types of cues is disrupted (Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014). However, the weight of a cue may change from trial to trial, depending on the information it carries in that trial, rather than only on its reliability over many trials (Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014).

Besides these temporal components, SoA also depends on intentionality, the mental representation of the action goal, which is discussed in the next section.

#### 4.3 Intentional Action as a Core Component of SoA

Having examined the components of SoA, it is necessary to delve deeper into a construct that traverses all the theoretical models discussed: intentionality. Although the terms "intention" and "intentionality" are often used interchangeably, they designate distinct concepts. Intention refers to the mental state that precedes action, i.e. the goal or desired state that the individual aims to achieve through their action. On the other hand, intentionality, in the sense used here, refers to the quality of an action being guided by intention. Namely, this is the relationship between the mental intention and the actual unfolding of the action (Pacherie, 2008). In the context of SoA, intentionality is crucial: it determines whether the action is experienced as something I did or not. For example, if I accidentally knock a glass off the table, I am clearly its cause, yet the movement was not guided by any intention, and I may experience it as something that happened to me rather than something I did (Metcalfe & Greene, 2007). The following paragraphs consider how intention is represented in each of the models discussed above.

In the comparator model, intention is represented by the goal (or desired) state, one of the five representations of the motor control system, from which the action starts (Frith, Blakemore & Wolpert, 2000). The goal state specifies the desired final position of the body and is also called the motor intention (Pacherie, 2008). It is transformed into motor commands through the inverse model, and the efference copy of these commands generates the sensory prediction (Pacherie, 2008). Thus, intention is the starting point of the entire causal chain that leads to SoA. Although motor intentions are often not conscious, they may still contribute to SoA (Pacherie, 2008); what matters is that an intention is present: without an intention, there is no prediction, hence no comparison and, consequently, no agency (Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008).

As described in the section on Wegner's retrospective theory, Wegner (2002; Wegner & Wheatley, 1999) proposed three criteria for the experience of agency: priority, exclusivity, and consistency. Among these, consistency is the criterion most directly relevant to the present work: it determines whether the observed outcome is attributed to the self or to an external cause. When the outcome matches the intention, the causal inference is confirmed; when it does not, it is weakened (Metcalfe & Greene, 2007; Wegner & Wheatley, 1999). Thus, intentionality is not merely a precondition of action, but also a criterion for the retrospective evaluation of agency.

Finally, in the cue integration model, intention contributes to the prior probability that the self is the agent ($p(\text{self})$) and to the probability that the self produced a given outcome ($p(\text{outcome} \mid \text{self})$) (Moore & Haggard, 2008, 2010; Wen & Imamizu, 2022). The clearer and more precise the intention, the stronger the prior, and the more the agency inference can rely on it rather than on sensory feedback (Moore & Haggard, 2008). In this sense, intention functions as a prospective cue that modulates the interpretation of retrospective cues (action-outcome matching) (Chambon, Sidarus & Haggard, 2014).

Intentionality, however, is only one side of SoA: the intention has to be carried out through an action, and the properties of that action also shape the experience of agency.

#### Intentional actions and the range of motion

Action is one of the key ingredients and lies at the heart of the sense of agency (SoA). Intentional, or self-generated, actions are typically defined as movements that arise from internal processes rather than being triggered by external stimuli. Within this view, voluntary action is characterized by autonomy, flexibility in integrating distinct causal factors, and control over outcomes (Chambon, Sidarus, & Haggard, 2014).

All theoretical accounts related to SoA make room for action in their interpretation, but action plays its most significant role in the comparator model, which frames SoA as the output of a system that generates goals and monitors actions against them. As explained earlier, the comparator model holds that agency arises when the predicted consequences of an action match its actual sensory outcome (Chambon et al., 2014). Within this framework, deviations in how a action itself is perceived — whether temporal, spatial, or anatomical in nature — are called predictive errors and can disrupt SoA.

The role of movement itself within this predictive framework is illustrated further by work on different types of sensorimotor mismatch. As shown by Krugwasser, Harel, and Salomon (2019), using a virtual-hand paradigm, both temporal and spatial mismatches reduced SoA to a statistically equivalent degree, whereas an anatomical mismatch (e.g., the wrong finger moving) produced a categorically different, near all-or-nothing collapse in agency.

However, several studies suggest that SoA is more sensitive to outcome-related than to movement-related disruptions. David, Skoruppa, Gulberti, Schultz, and Engel (2016) introduced delays either to a virtual hand's own movement or to the auditory or visual outcome of a tap, and found that self-agency reports dropped off faster with delays to the outcome than to the movement itself, regardless of sensory modality — suggesting SoA prioritizes authorship over action consequences rather than precise movement monitoring. This contrasts with Zama, Takahashi, and Shimada (2017), who manipulated trajectory and endpoint errors in a reaching task and found the opposite pattern: SoA correlated significantly with trajectory (movement) error but not with endpoint (outcome) error, once the error was explicitly noticed. The discrepancy between these findings appears to hinge on embodiment — David et al.'s virtual-hand setup may have masked small movement-related mismatches that a less embodied, cursor-based setup (as in Zama et al., or in Metcalfe, Eich, and Miele's, 2013, earlier proximal/distal work) would not. Despite this discrepancy, both studies underscore the central role that movement plays in shaping SoA.

Not all actions that elicit a sense of agency are strictly intentional, however. Moore, Wegner, and Haggard (2009) showed that even passive, involuntary movements can produce action-effect binding paired with an external cue consistent with the movement's effect, and that this cue-driven modulation was in fact stronger for involuntary than for voluntary movements. Critically, though, this effect still relied on the presence of actual bodily movement. When movement is removed altogether, the picture changes: Antusch, Custers, Marien, and Aarts (2021) delivered a purely tactile sensation to the fingertip, followed by a tone, with no motor component at all. Rather than the temporal binding that normally accompanies action-effect pairs, they found a robust temporal repulsion effect. Providing participants with full predictive knowledge of the sensation's identity and timing weakened this repulsion but never reversed it into binding — suggesting that intentional motor action holds a distinct status in producing SoA that sheer prediction cannot substitute for.

Together, these findings frame an open question: if movement itself — independent of outcome, embodiment, or predictive knowledge — is central to SoA, what specific properties of that movement matter? One candidate that has received comparatively little direct attention is the range of motion of the action itself, that is, whether the magnitude or amplitude of a movement modulates the strength of the resulting sense of agency. While the literature reviewed above establishes that a movement's presence, timing, and accuracy all shape sense of agency, its amplitude specifically remains up to my knowledge unexplored.

#### 4.4 Range of Motion and Motor Involvement

After we discussed the fact that intentionality is a central component of SoA and that AOC directly manipulates it, it is necessary to examine a second central experimental variable of this thesis: range of motion. This variable refers to the spatial and temporal extent of the motor action, i.e. how much the body moves, how long the movement trajectory is, and how much time the motor system is involved in executing the action. In the present design, range of motion varies along a gradient from keypress (pressing a button, where we have a minimal, discrete movement) to mouse movement (moving the cursor, which is a moderate, continuous movement) to touchscreen pointing (touching the screen, that is a maximal, direct, and embodied movement).

The relationship between motor involvement and SoA is complex and not unidirectional. On the one hand, one might assume that greater motor involvement goes into more movement, which will cause more effort, and hence more proprioceptive signals that would lead to stronger SoA. On the other hand, the literature suggests that the relationship is not so simple. Braun et al. (2018) showed that SoA and SoO are distinct constructs, and increased motor involvement may affect them differently. Furthermore, cue integration theory (Moore & Haggard, 2010) suggests that the reliability of cues matters more than their quantity: a simple but precise and fluent action (such as a keypress) may produce stronger SoA than a complex but noisy action (such as mouse movement or pointing).

The body is not merely a vehicle for action, but also a source of signals that contribute to SoA. Proprioceptive signals, tactile feedback, and muscular sensations provide information about the unfolding of the action that is integrated with sensorimotor predictions to generate SoA (Tsakiris et al., 2007; Apps & Tsakiris, 2014). The more the body is involved in the action, the more signals are available. However, these signals can also be noisy, i.e. proprioceptive and temporal variability of large movements may reduce the precision of prediction and, implicitly, may weaken SoA. This tension between the quantity and quality of signals is central to the Hypothesis 3 prediction.

An important distinction in the motor literature is the one between discrete and continuous actions. A particular aspect of continuous actions is eye-hand coupling: during visually guided movements, gaze and hand (or cursor) are coupled. Johansson et al. However, the coupling between gaze and cursor is not one of simple simultaneous tracking. Huang, White and Buscher (2012) showed that, in web search tasks, the cursor lags gaze by approximately 700 ms: the user looks at an element first, then moves the cursor toward it. Moreover, the inverse hypothesis — that gaze follows the cursor — was not supported by the data. This suggests that eye-hand coupling is an anticipatory process: gaze guides future action, rather than tracking it in real time. In Experiment 2, this temporal dynamic is relevant: the participant looks toward the selected area before moving the cursor, and the avatar's gaze moves in the same direction — but with a different temporal sequence. This nuance links Experiment 2 to the literature on predictive processing (Apps & Tsakiris, 2014) and on prospective cues (Chambon & Haggard, 2012).

The specific relevance of eye-hand coupling for Experiment 2 lies in the fact that the participant moves the cursor toward an area of the screen to select the avatar's gaze direction. Due to gaze-cursor coupling, the participant's gaze is directed toward the selected area, and the avatar's gaze moves in the same direction — creating a situation of self-other mirroring in which one's own behaviour (gaze) and the other's behaviour (avatar's gaze) are aligned. This situation is theoretically distinct from Experiment 1, where the keypress does not involve spatial coordination of gaze. In summary, this means that, in Experiment 2, the participant's gaze is already directed toward the selected area before the cursor reaches it, a form of gaze pre-orienting that may create a stronger prediction than the cursor movement itself.

### 5. Measurement of SoA

#### 5.1 Explicit Measures

SoA can be measured in two distinct ways (Reddy, 2022). In explicit measures, participants verbally report whether they consider they have control over their actions or whether they (or someone else) are the causal agent of observed actions (Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen, 2008a). Although verbal reports contain rich information, they can be influenced by subjective criteria, biases, or decision-making disturbances. For example, a liberal criterion can lead to more "yes" responses compared to conservative criterion, but the liberal criterion is not necessarily linked to a stronger SoA (Wen & Imamizu, 2022). For this reason, researchers have developed indirect (implicit) measures.

#### 5.2 Implicit Measures and Intentional Binding

In implicit measures, researchers study how voluntary actions modify perceptual processes — such as temporal, visual, tactile, or auditory perception — without asking the participant to explicitly reflect on their agency (Haggard, Clark & Kalogeras, 2002; Moore & Obhi, 2012). The most used implicit measure is intentional binding (or temporal binding). It refers to the perceived compression of the temporal interval between a voluntary action and its sensory effect: when a person performs a voluntary action that produces a sensory effect, the interval between the two events is perceived as shorter than the objective interval.

More precisely, two temporal shifts occur simultaneously: (1) the action is perceived as occurring later than in reality (action binding), and (2) the effect is perceived as occurring earlier than in reality (effect binding). The two events thus approach each other in subjective time. Action binding and effect binding reflect different aspects of the intentional binding effect and are useful for separating anomalies in action perception from those in sensory feedback perception. For example, patients with Parkinson's disease show abnormal action binding, while their effect binding is like that of healthy participants (Wen & Imamizu, 2022). Importantly, the intentional binding effect is not observed when the action is involuntary — for example, when it is triggered by brain stimulation (Wen & Imamizu, 2022). This suggests that binding requires a volitional component, not just a simple temporal contingency.

The classical method for measuring intentional binding uses the Libet clock: a circular dial on which a dot rotates continuously. The participant must report the position of the dot at the moment they executed the action and at the moment they perceived the effect (Libet et al., 1983; Haggard et al., 2002). By comparing the reported position with the objective position, the subjective shift is calculated. This measure is considered implicit because it does not ask the participant to explicitly reflect on their agency.

#### 5.3 Dissociations Between Explicit and Implicit Measures

Although both types of measures are considered valid, they are not interchangeable. Wen, Yamashita, and Asama (2015) experimentally demonstrated that implicit and explicit measures can be dissociated: in a study that manipulated action-effect delay (0–1000 ms) and arousal (via stimulus colour), longer delay significantly reduced explicit agency ratings, but increased the intentional binding effect. Moreover, arousal amplified binding, but did not influence explicit ratings. This double dissociation suggests that the two types of measures reflect different aspects of SoA.

A similar dissociation was reported by Grynszpan, Sahaï et al. (2019) in a joint action study. Participants jointly manipulated two connected haptic devices, believing that their partner was always another person, although sometimes the partner was replaced by a robot. The results showed a striking dissociation: explicit agency judgments were higher with the robot partner, while intentional binding occurred only with the human partner.

A recent critical review of the literature on agency in joint action (Le Besnerais et al., 2024) confirms that implicit and explicit measures of SoA can diverge systematically. The authors emphasize that intentional binding and explicit agency judgments reflect different levels of processing and recommend using both types of measures for a complete characterization of SoA.

### 6. Action-Effect Binding: The Mechanism Linking Action to SoA

A central concept linking the AOC manipulation to SoA is action-effect binding — the tendency to mentally associate actions with their anticipated sensory effects. According to ideomotor theory, actions are not mentally represented solely by their movements, but by the sensory effects they produce (Hommel, Müsseler, Aschersleben & Prinz, 2001). When a person learns to press a button to produce a sound, the mental representation of the action already includes the anticipated sound. This anticipated representation of the effect is activated before the action is executed and guides response selection and preparation.

Kühn, Brass and Haggard (2011) experimentally demonstrated that this action-effect link is directly relevant to SoA. In a series of studies, they manipulated the consistency between the participant's action and the observed effect, measuring both explicit SoA and intentional binding. The results showed that when the observed effect matched the anticipated effect, participants reported stronger SoA; when the effect was inconsistent with the anticipation, SoA was reduced. Moreover, this modulation was present even when participants had no explicit intention to produce a specific effect — suggesting that action-effect binding operates at an automatic, pre-reflective level.

Action-effect binding is also limited in time: SoA operates within a limited time window, and the longer the interval between an action and its effect, the less likely people are to judge that they caused the effect (Engbert et al., 2008; Farrer et al., 2013; Wen et al., 2015). In the present paradigm, the avatar's gaze shift followed the participant's response immediately and the target appeared at the end of the 210-ms gaze movement, so that both the gaze shift and the target fell within this action–effect window (Petkova & Janyan, 2026; Volosin et al., 2025).

Applied to the design of the present study, action-effect binding provides the cognitive mechanism that explains why the AOC manipulation works. In the consistent condition, the participant intends a direction, activates the anticipated representation of the effect, and the observed effect matches this anticipation — which confirms the action-effect link and supports SoA. In the inconsistent condition, the participant intends a direction, but the observed effect is opposite — which violates the anticipated link, generates a prediction error, and reduces SoA. In the control condition, there is no action from the participant, hence no anticipated representation of the effect — and SoA is absent or minimal.

Thus, action-effect binding is not merely a theoretical phenomenon, but the mechanism through which AOC manipulates SoA. It explains why the consistency between action and effect is crucial: because actions are mentally represented in terms of their effects, and the match between the anticipated and observed effect is the condition for SoA to arise.

### 7. Clinical Implications: Schizophrenia and Autism

<!-- FIX 37: Outline: pathology in 1-2 sentences, and it names OCD (inflated SoA), which is missing. The draft has three paragraphs; decide the length. The schizophrenia/Parkinson/Tourette claims need /lit-check (Voss et al. 2010 in particular). -->

Disturbances of SoA have been documented in various clinical conditions. In schizophrenia, patients may attribute their own actions to external agents or, conversely, may feel that they caused events over which they had no control — a phenomenon known as delusions of control (Frith, 2014; for a review, see Braun et al., 2018). A recent systematic review of studies investigating intentional binding (IB) in neuropsychiatric disorders (Moccia et al., 2024) offers a comprehensive perspective on how SoA is disrupted across clinical conditions. In schizophrenia, patients show a hyper-binding effect — an implicit over-attribution of the sensory consequences of movement to the self (Haggard et al., 2003; Voss et al., 2010). This over-attribution is consistent with neurocognitive theories that emphasize the role of impaired sensorimotor predictions in the genesis of positive symptoms (Moccia et al., 2024). In Parkinson's disease, hyper-binding appears only in patients taking dopaminergic medication or reporting impulsive-compulsive behaviors, suggesting that dopamine plays a critical role in modulating SoA (Moore, Schneider et al., 2010; Ricciardi et al., 2017). Conversely, in Tourette syndrome and functional movement disorders, IB is reduced — suggesting an under-attribution of agency (Kranick et al., 2013; Zapparoli et al., 2020).

Less studied, but equally relevant to the present work, is the possible link between SoA and autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Individuals with autism often show particularities in processing social signals and in integrating sensory information, which could also affect the sense of agency. Although the literature is still limited, a few studies have reported subtle differences in SoA in individuals with autism, especially regarding the integration of sensory and motor cues (Zalla & Sperduti, 2015). For example, individuals with autism may have difficulty discriminating between their own actions and those of others, especially when sensory feedback is ambiguous or delayed (Grynszpan, Nadel et al., 2012).

These clinical observations converge on a broader theoretical question: is SoA connected to theory of mind (ToM), i.e. the capacity to attribute mental states to others? Several lines of evidence suggest that it is. Both SoA and ToM require self–other distinction: the ability to differentiate between one's own actions and mental states and those of others (Gallese, 2003; Decety & Sommerville, 2003). At the neural level, the temporo-parietal junction (TPJ) is implicated in both SoA and ToM (Schilbach et al., 2012; Saxe et al. 2004). Furthermore, the capacity to control another's gaze, which is a form of social agency, involves both SoA and ToM (Stephenson et al., 2018). Autism offers a natural test of this link: individuals with autism show well-documented difficulties in ToM (Baron-Cohen, 1995) and subtle differences in SoA (Zalla & Sperduti, 2015). If SoA and ToM are connected, then these differences should be correlated — a prediction that has implications for understanding both basic mechanisms and clinical profiles. This thesis examines this link indirectly, by testing whether AOC, a manipulation of the consistency between action and outcome, modulates the gaze cueing effect, which is itself a foundational component of joint attention and ToM.

## Part IV — Does Agency Change Perception and Attention? And Can It Extend to Social Cues?

In the previous section, we examined the concept of sense of agency (SoA) and its components, providing a theoretical framework for understanding how agency can modulate gaze cueing. We established that SoA is a multifaceted construct that can be experimentally manipulated through the consistency between action and effect (AOC). But what happens after agency is generated? What are its consequences for perceptual and attentional processes? This section addresses these questions, moving from the antecedents of SoA to its consequences. We will examine four lines of evidence showing that agency is not merely an epiphenomenon of action, but an active factor that shapes how we perceive and orient our attention. And as a conclusion, we will ask whether this effect extends to social stimuli such as gaze.

The first line of evidence comes from intentional binding (IB). As discussed in section 5.2, IB refers to the perceived compression of the temporal interval between a voluntary action and its sensory effect. Haggard, Clark and Kalogeras (2002) demonstrated that voluntary actions are perceived as shifted later in time, and their effects as shifted earlier — the two events "approach" each other in subjective time. This effect does not occur for involuntary movements, suggesting that IB reflects a process specific to agency. Moreover, IB is not merely a perceptual curiosity: it is considered the perceptual signature of agency (Haggard, 2017). When action and effect are causally linked, the brain integrates them temporally, and this integration can influence how subsequent events are processed. Thus, IB provides the first evidence that agency changes perception — in this case, the perception of time.

However, a crucial question arises: does IB coincide with the explicit sense of agency? If the two measures were unrelated, IB could not be considered a valid marker of agency. Imaizumi and Tanno (2019) addressed this question directly. In two experiments, participants pressed a key that triggered an auditory (Experiment 1) or visual (Experiment 2) outcome after variable delays (100–900 ms). In each trial, participants rated their sense of agency over the outcome and estimated the action–outcome interval. The results showed that longer delays decreased both agency ratings and intentional binding. Critically, the authors found intra-individual correlations between agency ratings and intentional binding on a trial-by-trial basis in both outcome modalities — that is, when a participant felt stronger agency on a given trial, they also showed stronger temporal binding on that same trial. This finding is important because it demonstrates that IB and explicit SoA are not dissociable artefacts, but two manifestations of the same underlying process. Moreover, it validates the use of IB as an implicit measure of agency in paradigms that manipulate action–outcome consistency, such as the AOC manipulation used in the present study.

But what determines the strength of IB? Beyond the mere presence of voluntary action, the degree of control over the outcome appears to be critical. Beck, Di Costa and Haggard (2017) manipulated the extent to which participants could control the intensity of a somatosensory outcome. In one condition, participants chose between two actions that differed in their probability of producing a high- or low-intensity outcome. In another condition, action selection was meaningless with respect to the outcome. The results showed that both action binding and outcome binding increased when participants had probabilistic control over the outcome. This finding demonstrates that intentional binding is sensitive not only to the presence of a voluntary action, but also to the degree of control the agent has over the outcome — a key component of the AOC manipulation in the present study.

The second line of evidence comes from sensory attenuation. Blakemore, Frith and Wolpert (1999) showed that self-produced stimuli — such as the tactile sensation of one's own hand touching an object — are perceived as less intense than identical externally produced stimuli. This effect is interpreted because of sensorimotor prediction: the brain "subtracts" the anticipated sensory consequences of its own action, thereby reducing the neural response to self-produced stimuli (Blakemore et al., 2000; Frith, Blakemore & Wolpert, 2000). Sensory attenuation is not merely a perceptual curiosity: it shows that agency modifies the perceptual magnitude of events. If agency can reduce the intensity of tactile stimulus, it might also modulate the salience of a visual stimulus or a social cue. This line of evidence supports the idea that agency not only accompanies perception but also shapes it.

But why does sensory attenuation occur, and is it driven by motor action or by the sense of agency itself? Brown, Adams, Parees, Edwards and Friston (2013) provided a normative answer within the framework of active inference. According to their account, sensory attenuation is not an anomaly or a quirk of motor control, but a necessary consequence of the way action and perception are coupled. In active inference, movement is produced by descending proprioceptive predictions that are fulfilled by classical reflex arcs. However, for these predictions to prevail over sensory evidence, the precision of sensory prediction errors must be attenuated — otherwise, the sensory evidence that one is not moving would override the descending predictions, and movement would be precluded. Sensory attenuation is therefore the Bayes-optimal withdrawal of precise sensory evidence during movement, equivalent to "attending away" from the consequences of self-made acts.

This account has two important implications for the present work. First, it demonstrates that sensory attenuation and agency attribution are intimately linked: the same mechanism that allows movement to occur also determines whether a sensation is attributed to the self or to an external cause. When attenuation fails, like in the case of schizophrenia, the force-matching illusion disappears, and patients may develop delusions of control, falsely attributing their own actions to external agents (Brown et al., 2013; Shergill et al., 2005). This clinical evidence shows that agency is not merely a cognitive label attached to action, but a perceptual phenomenon grounded in the modulation of sensory precision. Second, it provides a unifying theoretical framework that connects the predictive processing model discussed in section 3.4 to the empirical phenomena of sensory attenuation and force-matching. If agency modulates perception through precision weighting, then it is plausible that the same mechanism modulates the salience of social cues — including gaze.

However, a crucial question remains: is sensory attenuation driven by motor action, by the sense of agency over the outcome, or by both? Han et colleagues (2021) addressed this question directly by dissociating motor action from sense of agency. In their paradigm, participants could control whether a sound would be presented by means of a willed inaction: if they chose not to press a button by a certain time, the sound was played; if they pressed, it was not. Thus, participants had complete control over the sound — and therefore a sense of agency over it — without performing any motor action. The results were striking: N1 suppression did not occur in the absence of a temporally coincident motor action, but Tb suppression did occur when participants had control over the sound. This dissociation demonstrates that sensory attenuation is not a unitary phenomenon. The N1 component appears to index motor-based prediction (requiring an efference copy), whereas the Tb component appears to index the sense of agency per se, independent of movement. This finding has two important implications. First, it confirms that agency can modulate perception independently of motor action — a stronger claim than the one supported by Brown et al. (2013), who linked attenuation primarily to movement. Second, it suggests that the effects of agency on perception are component-specific: different aspects of perceptual processing are influenced by different factors.

Recent studies have shown that people can feel agency over social outcomes, including the behaviour of other agents. Despite these advances, there is a major gap in literature. Most studies linking agency to gaze cueing ask: “Gaze → Agency” — that is, how gaze following or eye contact influences SoA (Ulloa et al., 2019; Stephenson et al., 2018). However, the reverse direction — “Agency → Gaze cueing” — has not been directly tested. The question is: if an observer causes another's gaze (and especially if that causation is consistent with their intention), does this modulate the magnitude of the gaze cueing effect? This is the central question of this thesis. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has directly tested whether AOC over a gaze modulates the GCE. This thesis addresses this gap by experimentally testing whether action-outcome consistency (AOC) modulates the GCE in three experiments with increasing range of motion.

## Rationale for the Current Study

Literature establishes three things. First, the gaze cueing effect (GCE) is flexible and can be modulated by facial, observer, and contextual factors (Part II). Second, the sense of agency (SoA) changes perception and attention for non-social stimuli: it compresses perceived time (intentional binding), attenuates the intensity of self-produced stimuli (sensory attenuation), and orients attention toward locations where action has an effect (action-based attention) (Part IV). Third, people can feel agency over social outcomes, including over another’s gaze (Grynszpan et al., 2019; Stephenson et al., 2018).

What is missing is a direct test of whether agency, and in particular action-outcome consistency, modulates the GCE. Existing studies have manipulated either face characteristics, observer traits, or social context. None has manipulated the observer’s own immediately preceding action as the source of the gaze. This thesis directly addresses this gap.

Action-outcome consistency (AOC) is the central operational manipulation of the thesis. It determines whether intentionality is fulfilled, violated, or absent. In the consistent condition, the avatar’s gaze direction matches the participant’s intended direction; in the inconsistent condition, it is opposite; in the control condition, there is no action from the participant, hence no intention to cause a specific direction. Thus, AOC directly operationalises Wegner’s (2002) consistency criterion and manipulates the retrospective component of SoA (see section 4.3).

To verify whether the AOC manipulation indeed produces differences in SoA, the thesis uses explicit Likert ratings. Participants answer three questions after each set of trials: (1) Did I predict the avatar’s gaze direction? (2) Did I cause the avatar’s gaze direction? (3) Did I control the avatar’s gaze direction? These questions target distinct dimensions of SoA, namely prediction, causality, and control, and allow a direct comparison between AOC conditions. Although the design does not allow a direct trial-by-trial causal test, the ratings provide an explicit validation of the manipulation.

In all three experiments, the participant sees an avatar whose gaze direction is determined by their action. The main task is a rapid colour discrimination on a target that appears congruent or incongruent with the avatar’s gaze direction. The AOC manipulation has three levels: consistent, inconsistent, and control. Range of motion varies across experiments: keypress (Experiment 1), mouse movement (Experiment 2), and touchscreen pointing (Experiment 3). In Experiment 1, the participant presses a button; in Experiment 2, they move the cursor; in Experiment 3, they touch the screen directly. In all experiments, initiation time is recorded as a measure of the prospective component.

H1: The gaze cueing effect (RT incongruent − RT congruent) will be largest in the consistent condition, intermediate in the control condition, and smallest (or absent/reversed) in the inconsistent condition.

This prediction is based on the pilot study by Petkova and Janyan (2026), which showed a GCE of 48 ms in the consistent condition, 23 ms in the inconsistent condition, and 17 ms (not significant) in the control condition. If H1 is confirmed, it would demonstrate that agency, which is operationalised through AOC, modulates attentional orienting in response to gaze.

H2: SoA ratings will be highest in consistent condition, intermediate in control condition, and lowest in the inconsistent condition.

This prediction follows directly from the definition of AOC: consistency between action and outcome should produce the strongest SoA, inconsistency the weakest, and absence of action an intermediate or reduced SoA. H2 extends the pilot study, which did not measure SoA explicitly.

H3: The modulation of the GCE by AOC will increase as range of motion increases, namely keypress (Exp 1) \< mouse (Exp 2) \< pointing (Exp 3).

This prediction is based on the idea that greater motor involvement produces richer proprioceptive signals and potentially stronger SoA, which in turn could amplify the effect on the GCE. However, there is also an alternative prediction: if motor noise and temporal variability of large movements reduce prediction precision, then the AOC effect might be weakened in Experiments 2 and 3. This theoretical tension is explored empirically.

The three experiments test this hypothesis along a gradient of motor embodiment. Together, they provide a systematic test of how agency — and in particular action-outcome consistency — modulates the gaze cueing effect. In what follows, Chapter 2 describes the method, results, and discussion of each experiment.
