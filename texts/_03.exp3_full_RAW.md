# Experiment 3

## Method

Experiment 3 retained the within-subjects design used throughout this series, crossing gaze congruency (congruent, incongruent) with action consistency (control, consistent, inconsistent) (see Experiment 1 Method for the full factor definitions). What set it apart was the modality of the direction-selection response: rather than pressing a key or clicking a mouse, participants indicated their intended gaze direction by touching the screen directly.

Each trial was preceded by a fixation symbol — a cross ("+") or an asterisk ("\*") — one of which signaled a control trial and the other an action trial calling for a direction-selection response; which symbol carried which meaning was counterbalanced across participants. The face then appeared flanked by two bordered, unfilled rectangles, one to its left and one to its right, occupying the same on-screen positions that the color target would later fill during the target-discrimination phase. To choose the direction they wished to induce, participants simply touched one of the two rectangles on the touchscreen; because the response was made by direct touch, no cursor was involved at any point. The chosen direction was scored according to which rectangle was touched. Touches landing outside both rectangles went unregistered and had no effect, so that only a touch inside one of the two rectangles brought the response window to a close.

Explicit sense of agency was again assessed at the end of the procedure using the same three self-report questions (predict / control / caused).

### Procedure

Apart from the touchscreen response, the trial unfolded as in the earlier experiments (fixation, face presentation, 210 ms gaze movement across seven frames, target-discrimination response window, inter-trial interval — see Experiment 1 Procedure for the full step-by-step timeline and Figure 1). The initiation time — the interval between face onset and the direction-selection response, later entered as an ANCOVA covariate — was defined exactly as before: on control trials, where no response was required, this interval was replaced by the corresponding face-display duration. The target-discrimination response remained a keyboard press using the a/d button pair. Because the direction-selection response was now made by touch and never competed with the discrimination task for the same keys, this assignment required no counterbalancing.

The questionnaire phase (three Likert-scale questions, 7-point scale, 48 trials, 16 per condition) and the overall trial count (168 experimental trials, 28 per condition) matched those of the previous experiments.

### Participants

Sixty-two students took part in the experiment (15 males, 47 females; M = 24.23 years, SD = 6.16). Participation was incentivized through ECTS credits tied to accuracy performance, and all participants provided written informed consent and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision.

## Results

**Data Exclusion and Final Sample**

Data were initially collected from 62 participants, yielding 10,416 trials in total. Eight participants were excluded because their accuracy in the main experimental phase fell below 85%, leaving 54 participants and 9,072 trials.

A further three participants were excluded on the basis of the predefined questionnaire data-quality criteria — invariant responding across items, defined as a dominant response exceeding 75% of all ratings combined with a condition-level response range of 0.5 or less (Meade & Craig, 2012). Seven additional participants were flagged by the same rule but retained, as their response range exceeded 0.5 and thus indicated that they still discriminated between conditions. This left 51 participants, contributing 8,568 trials.

From these trials, 5.00% (428 trials) were removed as error trials, leaving 8,140 trials. Trials with a reaction time below 200 ms were then excluded as physiologically implausible for this task (27 trials, 0.33%, spread across 14 participants), leaving 8,113 trials. A further 4.72% (383 trials) were excluded because their remaining reaction times deviated by more than 2 standard deviations from the mean, calculated separately for each participant and condition, leaving 7,730 trials.

Two further participants (ss11, ss32) were excluded because their overall mean reaction time exceeded the group mean by more than 2 standard deviations (cutoff: 721.7 ms; participants' means: 784.8 ms and 745.4 ms).

The final analyses were therefore conducted on data from 49 participants.

**Questionary data**

**Descriptives**

As in Experiments 1 and 2, the three questions were answered on a seven-point Likert scale. Descriptive statistics are presented in Table X.

Ratings followed a broadly similar ordering to the previous two experiments — highest in the active conditions, lowest in control — but the gap between the consistent and inconsistent conditions, already reduced in Experiment 2, largely disappeared here. In the consistent condition, means were close together across all three items (causation M = 3.56, SD = 1.62; control M = 3.55, SD = 1.63; prediction M = 3.67, SD = 1.47). The inconsistent condition showed a similar clustering (causation M = 3.08, SD = 1.40; control M = 2.93, SD = 1.20; prediction M = 3.03, SD = 1.06). As in the previous two experiments, the control condition produced the lowest ratings overall, ranging from 2.07 (SD = 1.11) for causation to 2.82 (SD = 1.39) for prediction, with prediction again the most highly endorsed item in this condition.

**Analyses**

As in Experiments 1 and 2, ratings were submitted to a 3 (Question type: caused, controlled, predicted) × 3 (Action Consistency: consistent, inconsistent, control) repeated-measures ANOVA, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied to all within-subject terms with more than one degree of freedom. Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated departures from normality in five of the nine cells (most of the control condition and part of the inconsistent condition); as before, the analysis is reported as planned.

The ANOVA again revealed a significant main effect of Action Consistency, *F*(1.64, 78.71) = 29.13, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .378 — closely matching the effect size found in Experiment 2 (η²ₚ = .394) and confirming that the paradigm continued to manipulate explicit SoA successfully, albeit with a substantially smaller effect than in Experiment 1 (η²ₚ = .673). Unlike in Experiments 1 and 2, however, the main effect of Question type was not significant, *F*(1.87, 89.85) = 1.93, *p* = .153, η²ₚ = .039: collapsed across condition, the three questions no longer differed from one another in overall level of endorsement. The Action Consistency × Question type interaction remained significant, *F*(2.41, 115.46) = 6.27, *p* = .001, η²ₚ = .116, though this was the smallest interaction effect size observed across the three experiments (Experiment 1: η²ₚ = .403; Experiment 2: η²ₚ = .192). As before, both directions of this interaction were examined in turn, each as its own Holm-corrected family of simple effects.

**Does action consistency affect the three questions differently?**

Simple main effects of Action Consistency within each question type were all significant — causation, *F*(2, 96) = 26.33, *p* \< .001; control, *F*(2, 96) = 25.86, *p* \< .001; prediction, *F*(2, 96) = 13.44, *p* \< .001 — again replicating the same ranking of item sensitivity seen in the previous two experiments (causation ≈ control > prediction).

Unlike Experiment 2, but similar to Experiment 1, Holm-corrected pairwise comparisons showed that consistent and inconsistent trials differed significantly from each other for all three questions (causation, *p* = .003, *d* = 0.34; control, *p* = .001, *d* = 0.46; prediction, *p* = .003, *d* = 0.46). Both active conditions again exceeded control for causation (consistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 1.08; inconsistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.74) and for the control item (consistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.92; inconsistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.46). For prediction, the consistent condition exceeded control (*p* \< .001, *d* = 0.61), but — replicating the one exception already seen in Experiment 1 — inconsistent and control did not differ significantly (*p* = .159, *d* = 0.15).

**Do the three questions differ from each other within the same condition?**

The reverse simple main effects showed the clearest divergence from the previous two experiments: Question type had no detectable effect in either active condition — consistent, *F*(2, 96) = 0.24, *p* = .788; inconsistent, *F*(2, 96) = 0.32, *p* = .725 — compared with a significant effect in Experiment 1 (both *p* \< .001) and a partially attenuated effect in Experiment 2 (*p* = .057 and *p* = .017, respectively). As in both previous experiments, question type remained significant in the control condition, *F*(2, 96) = 11.82, *p* \< .001.

Because the simple effects in the consistent and inconsistent conditions were far from significant, pairwise comparisons were not examined for those two conditions. In the control condition, the reversal observed in Experiments 1 and 2 replicated for a third time: prediction was rated higher than both causation (*p* \< .001, *d* = −0.55) and control (*p* = .021, *d* = −0.39), with causation and control differing only marginally (*p* = .075, *d* = −0.16).

Taken together with Experiments 1 and 2, these results point to a consistent, monotonic decline across the three experiments in the questions' ability to differentiate from one another within a single active condition, alongside one unchanging exception: whenever participants were passive (the control condition), prediction was consistently the most highly endorsed item, in all three experiments without exception. **Reaction Time, Gaze Cueing, and Sense of Agency**

**Descriptive statistics**

Descriptive statistics were computed for reaction times (RTs) in the main experimental phase as a function of gaze congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) and action consistency (consistent, inconsistent, control). Means and standard deviations for each condition are presented in Table X.

The fastest responses were observed in the congruent-consistent condition (M = 504.52 ms, SD = 91.90), as in Experiments 1 and 2, whereas the slowest were again observed in the incongruent-control condition (M = 536.51 ms, SD = 84.90).

The mean congruency effect (incongruent minus congruent, computed per participant) was 25.08 ms (SD = 33.02) for consistent trials and 18.94 ms (SD = 31.50) for control trials — both in the expected direction. As in Experiment 2, the inconsistent condition showed the reversed pattern, with a mean difference of −12.79 ms (SD = 45.76), i.e., numerically faster responses on incongruent than congruent trials. As reported below, this recurring reversal is again backed by a significant interaction, mirroring Experiment 2 and diverging from Experiment 1 (where the inconsistent-condition effect was small but still positive, 4.99 ms, and the interaction was not significant).

Within gaze-congruent trials, RTs were fastest in the consistent condition (M = 504.52 ms, SD = 91.90), followed by control (M = 517.57 ms, SD = 83.05) and inconsistent (M = 520.76 ms, SD = 83.24). Within gaze-incongruent trials, the ordering resembled Experiment 2: inconsistent trials were fastest (M = 507.97 ms, SD = 95.87), followed by consistent (M = 529.61 ms, SD = 94.90) and control (M = 536.51 ms, SD = 84.90).

**rANOVA**

As in Experiment 2, data were averaged by participant and entered into a repeated-measures ANOVA with gaze congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) and action consistency (consistent, inconsistent, control) as within-subject factors, Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied to all within-subject terms with more than one degree of freedom, and Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons.

There was a significant main effect of gaze congruency, *F*(1, 48) = 13.72, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .222, replicating the gaze-cueing effect found in Experiments 1 and 2.

The main effect of action consistency was also significant, *F*(1.49, 71.40) = 3.97, *p* = .034, η²ₚ = .076 — the weakest of the three experiments (cf. η²ₚ = .220 in Experiment 2). None of the Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons (collapsed across gaze congruency) reached significance: consistent vs. inconsistent (*p* = 1.0), consistent vs. control (*p* = .202), and inconsistent vs. control (*p* = .066, the closest to significance, with inconsistent trials numerically faster). The omnibus effect is therefore significant, but no single pairwise contrast is individually reliable once corrected.

Critically, the Gaze Congruency × Action Consistency interaction was again significant, *F*(1.71, 82.23) = 13.43, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .219 — closely matching the size of the interaction in Experiment 2 (η²ₚ = .206) and, as in Experiment 2, diverging from Experiment 1, where the interaction did not reach significance.

Decomposing this interaction, the simple congruency effect (incongruent minus congruent) was examined within each level of action consistency, Bonferroni-corrected as its own family of three comparisons. The pattern replicated Experiment 2 closely: the congruency effect was significant and in the expected direction within the consistent condition (25.08 ms, *SE* = 4.72, *t*(48) = 5.32, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.27) and within the control condition (18.94 ms, *SE* = 4.50, *t*(48) = 4.21, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.23), but in the inconsistent condition the effect was again numerically reversed and non-significant (−12.79 ms, *SE* = 6.54, *t*(48) = 1.96, *p* = .056, *d* = 0.14). As in Experiment 2, the standard gaze-cueing advantage held reliably whenever participants either made a matching choice or made no choice at all, but broke down — and numerically reversed — specifically when the gaze moved opposite to the participant's chosen direction.

This is now the second consecutive experiment (of the two in which the RT interaction has been tested) showing this same qualitative pattern: a reliable congruency effect in the consistent and control conditions, and a non-significant, numerically reversed congruency effect in the inconsistent condition. Given Experiment 1 showed neither the reversal nor a significant interaction, this looks like a genuine divergence between the keypress-response experiment and the two experiments with spatially extended direction-selection responses (mouse click, touchscreen), rather than a smooth gradient across all three — worth flagging for the cross-experiment RT discussion once it is written, alongside the parallel observation already noted for the questionnaire data.

As in Experiments 1 and 2, a repeated-measures ANCOVA was conducted with mean initiation time as a covariate, mean-centered, restricted to the two active conditions (consistent, inconsistent); the control condition was excluded for the same reason as before.

Gaze congruency was marginal after controlling for initiation time, *F*(1, 47) = 3.21, *p* = .080, η²ₚ = .064, and action consistency was not significant, *F*(1, 47) = 0.46, *p* = .501, η²ₚ = .010. The action × initiation-time interaction was not significant, *F*(1, 47) = 0.01, *p* = .919, η²ₚ \< .001, but the gaze × initiation-time interaction was, *F*(1, 47) = 4.40, *p* = .041, η²ₚ = .086, indicating that the size of the congruency effect itself varied with how quickly participants initiated their action. The Gaze × Action interaction remained highly significant in this reduced, covariate-adjusted model, *F*(1, 47) = 18.25, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .280 -- consistent with, and numerically larger than, the interaction already found in the plain rANOVA above. The three-way interaction was not significant, *F*(1, 47) = 2.76, *p* = .103, η²ₚ = .055.

This matches Experiment 2 rather than Experiment 1: in both Experiments 2 and 3, the Gaze × Action interaction is robust on average and does not depend on initiation time (non-significant three-way term), whereas in Experiment 1 the interaction was not significant on average and only appeared as a function of initiation time (significant three-way term, non-significant interaction itself). Experiment 3 additionally shows a significant gaze × initiation-time relationship that Experiment 2 does not, indicating that the size of the basic congruency effect (independent of consistency) scales with initiation time here specifically.

**Correlation**

Pearson correlations among the six RT cells and mean initiation time were computed. As in Experiments 1 and 2, all six RT conditions intercorrelated strongly and positively (*r* = .77–.91, all *p* \< .001), indicating stable individual differences in general response speed rather than any condition-specific pattern.

As in Experiment 2, initiation time correlated strongly and significantly with RT in every condition (*r* = .51–.69, all *p* \< .001) -- markedly stronger than in Experiment 1, and consistent with a touchscreen point-and-select response sharing substantial psychomotor-speed variance with the subsequent target-discrimination RT, similar to the mouse-click response in Experiment 2.

**Bin Analysis**

As in Experiments 1 and 2, congruent- and incongruent-trial RTs were Vincentized separately within each action-consistency condition (sorted, split into 5 equal-size quantile bins per participant) and submitted to a 2 (Gaze) × 5 (bin) repeated-measures ANOVA, run separately per condition.

The Gaze main effect was significant in the consistent condition (F(1, 48) = 26.37, p \< .001, η²ₚ = .355) and the control condition (F(1, 48) = 17.81, p \< .001, η²ₚ = .271), and significant in the inconsistent condition (F(1, 48) = 4.64, p = .036, η²ₚ = .088) -- broadly the same pattern as Experiments 1 and 2. The bin main effect was again extremely large in all three conditions. **The Gaze × Bin interaction was not significant in any condition**, though the consistent condition came closest of any condition checked so far across all three experiments (GG F(1.79, 86.11) = 2.11, p = .132; inconsistent: GG F(1.71, 82.23) = 0.56, p = .545; control: GG F(1.89, 90.64) = 0.94, p = .389).

Descriptively, the consistent condition's congruency gap declines across the first four bins (28, 25, 20, 17 ms) before jumping back up in the slowest bin (34 ms) -- a non-monotonic shape, not a clean early-and-fading pattern, and this is the condition/experiment combination that came closest to (but did not reach) a significant interaction. The inconsistent condition again shows its reversal holding steady across the whole distribution (−13, −10, −10, −17, −19 ms), matching Experiment 2. Control shows a mild decline (24 ms down to 13 ms), matching the direction seen in both other experiments. As in Experiments 1 and 2, the bin analysis provides no statistical evidence that the congruency effect is concentrated in a particular part of the RT distribution.
