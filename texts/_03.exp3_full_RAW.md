# Experiment 3

## Method

Experiment 3 retained the within-subjects design used throughout this series, crossing gaze congruency (congruent, incongruent) with action consistency (control, consistent, inconsistent) (see Experiment 1 Method for the full factor definitions). What set it apart was the modality of the direction-selection response: rather than pressing a key or clicking a mouse, participants indicated their intended gaze direction by touching the screen directly.

Each trial was preceded by a fixation symbol — a cross ("+") or an asterisk ("\*") — one of which signaled a control trial and the other an action trial calling for a direction-selection response; which symbol carried which meaning was counterbalanced across participants. The face then appeared flanked by two bordered, unfilled rectangles, one to its left and one to its right, occupying the same on-screen positions that the color target would later fill during the target-discrimination phase. To choose the direction they wished to induce, participants simply touched one of the two rectangles on the touchscreen; because the response was made by direct touch, no cursor was involved at any point. The chosen direction was scored according to which rectangle was touched. Touches landing outside both rectangles went unregistered and had no effect, so that only a touch inside one of the two rectangles brought the response window to a close.

Explicit sense of agency was again assessed at the end of the procedure using the same three self-report questions (predict / control / caused).

### Procedure

Apart from the touchscreen response, the trial unfolded as in the earlier experiments (fixation, face presentation, 210 ms gaze movement across seven frames, target-discrimination response window, inter-trial interval — see Experiment 1 Procedure for the full step-by-step timeline and Figure 1). The initiation time — the interval between face onset and the direction-selection response, later entered as an ANCOVA covariate — was defined exactly as before: on control trials, where no response was required, this interval was replaced by the corresponding face-display duration. The target-discrimination response remained a keyboard press using the a/d button pair. Because the direction-selection response was now made by touch and never competed with the discrimination task for the same keys, this assignment required no counterbalancing.

The questionnaire phase (three Likert-scale questions, 7-point scale, 48 trials, 16 per condition) and the overall trial count (168 experimental trials, 28 per condition) matched those of the previous experiments.

### Participants

Sixty-two students took part in the experiment. ## Results

**Data Exclusion and Final Sample**

Data were initially collected from 62 participants, yielding 10,416 trials in total. Eight participants were excluded because their accuracy in the main experimental phase fell below 85%, leaving 54 participants and 9,072 trials.

A further three participants were excluded on the basis of the predefined questionnaire data-quality criteria — invariant responding across items, defined as a dominant response exceeding 75% of all ratings combined with a condition-level response range of 0.5 or less (Meade & Craig, 2012). Seven additional participants were flagged by the same rule but retained, as their response range exceeded 0.5 and thus indicated that they still discriminated between conditions. This left 51 participants, contributing 8,568 trials.

From these trials, 5.00% (428 trials) were removed as error trials, leaving 8,140 trials. A further 4.88% (397 trials) were excluded because their reaction times deviated by more than 2 standard deviations from the mean, calculated separately for each participant and condition, leaving 7,743 trials.

Two further participants were excluded because their overall mean reaction time exceeded the group mean by more than 2 standard deviations (cutoff: 723.2 ms).

The final analyses were therefore conducted on data from 49 participants.

**Questionary data**

**Descriptives**

As in Experiments 1 and 2, the three questions were answered on a seven-point Likert scale. Descriptive statistics are presented in Table X.

Ratings followed a broadly similar ordering to the previous two experiments — highest in the active conditions, lowest in control — but the gap between the consistent and inconsistent conditions, already reduced in Experiment 2, largely disappeared here. In the consistent condition, means were close together across all three items (causation M = 3.56, SD = 1.62; control M = 3.55, SD = 1.63; prediction M = 3.67, SD = 1.47). The inconsistent condition showed a similar clustering (causation M = 3.08, SD = 1.40; control M = 2.93, SD = 1.20; prediction M = 3.03, SD = 1.06). As in the previous two experiments, the control condition produced the lowest ratings overall, ranging from 2.07 (SD = 1.11) for causation to 2.82 (SD = 1.39) for prediction, with prediction again the most highly endorsed item in this condition.

**Analyses**

As in Experiments 1 and 2, ratings were submitted to a 3 (Question type: caused, controlled, predicted) × 3 (Action Consistency: consistent, inconsistent, control) repeated-measures ANOVA, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied to all within-subject terms with more than one degree of freedom. Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated departures from normality in five of the nine cells (most of the control condition and part of the inconsistent condition); as before, the analysis is reported as planned.

The ANOVA again revealed a significant main effect of Action Consistency, *F*(1.64, 78.71) = 29.13, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .378 — closely matching the effect size found in Experiment 2 (η²ₚ = .394) and confirming that the paradigm continued to manipulate explicit SoA successfully, albeit with a substantially smaller effect than in Experiment 1 (η²ₚ = .673). Unlike in Experiments 1 and 2, however, the main effect of Question type was not significant, *F*(1.87, 89.85) = 1.93, *p* = .153, η²ₚ = .039: collapsed across condition, the three questions no longer differed from one another in overall level of endorsement. The Action Consistency × Question type interaction remained significant, *F*(2.41, 115.46) = 6.27, *p* = .001, η²ₚ = .116, though this was the smallest interaction effect size observed across the three experiments (Experiment 1: η²ₚ = .403; Experiment 2: η²ₚ = .192). As before, both directions of this interaction were examined in turn, each as its own Holm-corrected family of simple effects.

**Does action consistency affect the three questions differently?**

Simple main effects of Action Consistency within each question type were all significant — causation, *F*(2, 96) = 26.33, *p* \< .001; control, *F*(2, 96) = 25.86, *p* \< .001; prediction, *F*(2, 96) = 13.44, *p* \< .001 — again replicating the same ranking of item sensitivity seen in the previous two experiments (causation ≈ control > prediction).

Unlike Experiment 2, but similar to Experiment 1, Holm-corrected pairwise comparisons showed that consistent and inconsistent trials differed significantly from each other for all three questions (causation, *p* = .003, *d* = 0.34; control, *p* = .001, *d* = 0.46; prediction, *p* = .003, *d* = 0.46). Both active conditions again exceeded control for causation (consistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 1.08; inconsistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.74) and for the control item (consistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.92; inconsistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.46). For prediction, the consistent condition exceeded control (*p* \< .001, *d* = 0.61), but — replicating the one exception already seen in Experiment 1 — inconsistent and control did not differ significantly (*p* = .159, *d* = 0.15).

**Do the three questions differ from each other within the same condition?**

The reverse simple main effects showed the clearest divergence from the previous two experiments: Question type had no detectable effect in either active condition — consistent, *F*(2, 96) = 0.24, *p* = .788; inconsistent, *F*(2, 96) = 0.32, *p* = .725 — compared with a significant effect in Experiment 1 (both *p* \< .001) and a partially attenuated effect in Experiment 2 (*p* = .057 and *p* = .017, respectively). As in both previous experiments, question type remained significant in the control condition, *F*(2, 96) = 11.82, *p* \< .001.

Because the simple effects in the consistent and inconsistent conditions were far from significant, pairwise comparisons were not examined for those two conditions. In the control condition, the reversal observed in Experiments 1 and 2 replicated for a third time: prediction was rated higher than both causation (*p* \< .001, *d* = −0.55) and control (*p* = .021, *d* = −0.39), with causation and control differing only marginally (*p* = .075, *d* = −0.16).

Taken together with Experiments 1 and 2, these results point to a consistent, monotonic decline across the three experiments in the questions' ability to differentiate from one another within a single active condition, alongside one unchanging exception: whenever participants were passive (the control condition), prediction was consistently the most highly endorsed item, in all three experiments without exception.
