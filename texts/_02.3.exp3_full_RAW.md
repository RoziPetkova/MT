# Experiment 3

## Method

Experiment 3 used the same design and procedure established in Experiments 1 and 2 (see Rationale setion for the full paradigm description). The only difference was the modality of the direction-selection response: instead of pressing a key or moving a mouse, participants were seated in front of a touchscreen monitor and indicated their intended gaze direction by touching one of the two rectangles to the left or right of the face. Unlike in Experiment 2, no mouse cursor was visible on the screen at any point (\[check with Armina]). Touches landing outside both rectangles were not registered as valid responses and were therefore ignored.

Explicit sense of agency was again assessed at the end of the procedure using the same three self-report questions (predict / control / caused).

### Participants

Sixty-two students took part in the experiment (15 males, 47 females; M = 24.23 years, SD = 6.16). Participation was incentivized through ECTS credits tied to accuracy performance, and all participants provided written informed consent and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision.

## Results

**Data Exclusion and Final Sample**

Data were initially collected from 62 participants. Eight participants were excluded due to lower accuracy (below 85%). A further three participants were excluded on the basis of the predefined questionnaire data-quality criteria — invariant responding across items, defined as a dominant response exceeding 75% of all ratings combined with a condition-level response range of 0.5 or less (Meade & Craig, 2012). This left 51 participants in the data set.

From the remaining trials, 5.00% (428 trials) were removed as errors; Additional another 0.33% (27 trials) were excluded as physiologically implausible (below 200 ms), leaving 8,113 RT trials. A further cut eliminated 4.72% (383 trials) because their remaining reaction times deviated by more than 2 standard deviations from the mean, calculated separately for each participant and condition, leaving 7,730 trials.

Two further participants (ss11, ss32) were excluded because their overall mean reaction time exceeded the group mean by more than 2 standard deviations (cutoff: 721.7 ms; participants' means: 784.8 ms and 745.4 ms). The final analyses were therefore conducted on data from 49 participants.

**Reaction Time, Gaze Cueing, and Sense of Agency**

**Descriptive statistics**

In general the pattern of results in Experiment 3 was following the one from Experiment 2. Means and standard deviations for each condition are presented in Table X.

The fastest responses were observed in the congruent-consistent condition (M = 504.52 ms, SD = 91.90) when selected direaction matches the gaze diretion and targer placement, as in Experiments 1 and 2. The slowest RTs were again observed in the incongruent-control condition (M = 536.51 ms, SD = 84.90) where no direction-selection (acton) was required and the gaze direction was opposite to the target location.

The mean congruency/GC effect (incongruent minus congruent, computed per participant) was 25.08 ms (SD = 33.02) for consistent trials and 18.94 ms (SD = 31.50) for control trials — both in the expected direction. As in Experiment 2, the inconsistent condition showed the reversed pattern, with a mean difference of −12.79 ms (SD = 45.76), i.e., numerically faster responses on incongruent than congruent trials.

Within gaze-congruent trials, RTs were fastest in the consistent condition (M = 504.52 ms, SD = 91.90), followed by control (M = 517.57 ms, SD = 83.05) and inconsistent (M = 520.76 ms, SD = 83.24) — a reversal of the ordering observed in the previous two experiments, where control was consistently the slowest condition. Within gaze-incongruent trials the ordering resembled Experiment 2: inconsistent trials were fastest (M = 507.97 ms, SD = 95.87), followed by consistent (M = 529.61 ms, SD = 94.90) and control (M = 536.51 ms, SD = 84.90).

**Statistical analyses**

A post-hoc power analysis (G\*Power 3.1.9.6) confirmed high power (power = 0.975) for the current sample (*N* = 49) to detect a small effect (*f* = 0.20); as in Experiments 1 and 2, Greenhouse–Geisser correction and Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons were applied throughout.

**rANOVA**

As in Experiment 1 and 2 a significant main effect of gaze congruency, *F*(1, 48) = 13.72, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .222,  was established. A further analysis showed that, as in Experiment 2, the CG effect (congruency effect) was present within the consistent (*F*(1, 48) = 28.28, *p* \< .001) and control conditions (*F*(1, 48) = 17.72, *p* \< .001), but in the inconsistent condition the effect (being numerically reversed) was only marginally below significance (*F*(1, 48) = 3.83, *p* = .056).

The main effect of action consistency again also reached significance *F*(1.49, 71.40) = 3.97, *p* = .034, η²ₚ = .076 — the weakest of the three experiments (cf. η²ₚ = .220 in Experiment 2). The simple main effect of action consistency was also examined within each level of gaze congruency: it was significant within congruent trials (*F*(2, 96) = 4.02, *p* = .021) and more strongly so within incongruent trials (*F*(2, 96) = 11.41, *p* \< .001). However none of the followup Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons reached significance: consistent vs. inconsistent (*p* = 1.0), consistent vs. control (*p* = .219), and inconsistent vs. control (*p* = .065). Taken together, these results indicate a genuine, if modest, effect of action consistency on RT, one that was more visible within incongruent than within congruent trials, but the pairwise comparisons did not indicate clearly its source.

Critically, as in the previous experiment, the Gaze Congruency × Action Consistency interaction was again significant, *F*(1.71, 82.23) = 13.43, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .219 — closely matching the size of the interaction in Experiment 2 (η²ₚ = .206). Diverging from Experiment 1, where the interaction did not reach significance. Post hoc comparisons conditioned on gaze congruency, however, revealed a somewhat different pattern than in Experiment 2. Within congruent trials, only the consistent-versus-inconsistent comparison reached significance, with consistent trials faster (MD = −16.24 ms, *SE* = 5.17, *t*(48) = −3.14, *p* = .009, *d* = −0.18); neither condition differed reliably from control (both *p* ≥ .116). Within incongruent trials, inconsistent trials were faster than both consistent (MD = 21.63 ms, *SE* = 5.37, *t*(48) = 4.03, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.24) and control (MD = −28.54 ms, *SE* = 6.93, *t*(48) = −4.12, *p* \< .001, *d* = −0.32), while consistent and control again did not differ from each other (*p* = .836). Notably, in both gaze conditions every significant comparison involved the inconsistent condition, whereas consistent and control never differed reliably from one another. This raises the possibility that the Gaze Congruency × Action Consistency interaction was driven primarily by the inconsistent condition — which was slowest within congruent trials but fastest within incongruent trials — rather than by a broader reorganization involving all three action conditions.

This is now the second consecutive experiment (of the two in which the RT interaction has been tested) showing this same qualitative pattern: a reliable congruency effect in the consistent and control conditions, and a non-significant, numerically reversed congruency effect in the inconsistent condition. Given Experiment 1 showed neither the reversal nor a significant interaction, this looks like a genuine divergence between the keypress-response experiment and the two experiments with spatially extended direction-selection responses (mouse click, touchscreen), rather than a smooth gradient across all three — worth flagging for the cross-experiment RT discussion once it is written, alongside the parallel observation already noted for the questionnaire data.

**ANCOVA Results**

As in Experiments 1 and 2, a repeated-measures ANCOVA was conducted with mean initiation time (mean-centered) included as a covariate.

Controlling for initiation time did not change the overall pattern of results: the main effects of Gaze, *F*(1, 47) = 14.71, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .238, and Action, *F*(1.58, 74.30) = 4.56, *p* = .020, η²ₚ = .088, and the Gaze × Action interaction, *F*(1.73, 81.53) = 13.59, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .224, all remained significant and closely matched their plain-rANOVA results reported above. The Action × covariate and Gaze × covariate interactions also reached significance (*F*(1.58, 74.30) = 8.19, *p* = .001; *F*(1, 47) = 4.47, *p* = .040, respectively); neither changes the interpretation of the effects reported above.

In the plain ANOVA above, the gaze congruency effect within the inconsistent condition was numerically reversed but not significant (*p* = .056). Once initiation time was controlled for in ANCOVA, this effect reached significance (MD = 12.79 ms, *SE* = 6.30, *t*(47) = 2.03, *p* = .048, *d* = 0.18). In other words, once general response-speed differences were accounted for, within inconsistent trials participants responded faster to targets on their chosen side (incongruent trials) than on the gazed-at side (congruent trials). This diverges from Experiment 2, where the equivalent ANCOVA-adjusted simple effect within the inconsistent condition remained non-significant (*p* = .072), closely matching its plain-ANOVA value (*p* = .070).

**Correlation**

Pearson correlations between initiation time and RT were computed for each condition. As in Experiment 2, initiation time correlated strongly and significantly with RT in every condition (*r* = .51–.69, all *p* \< .001), just as the mouse-click response did in Experiment 2, the touchscreen response shares much of its speed with the later target-discrimination RT.

Taken together, the RT results in Experiment 3 closely replicate Experiment 2: a standard gaze-cueing effect, a significant Gaze × Action Consistency interaction (absent in Experiment 1), and a congruency effect that held in the consistent and control conditions but broke down — and numerically reversed — in the inconsistent condition. The one departure from Experiment 2 only appeared once initiation time was controlled for: in Experiment 3, unlike Experiment 2, this reversed effect in the inconsistent condition became statistically reliable, with participants responding faster to targets on the side they had themselves selected than on the side the face actually gazed toward. One possible explanation regarding this specific result is that its plausible prediction errors elicited by inconsistent action outcomes compete with the processing of gaze cues, attenuating the gaze-cueing effect.

**Analyses**

The analysis followed the already established procedure — ratings were submitted to a 3 (Question type: caused, controlled, predicted) × 3 (Action Consistency: consistent, inconsistent, control) repeated-measures ANOVA, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied to all within-subject terms with more than one degree of freedom.

The ANOVA again revealed a significant main effect of Action Consistency, *F*(1.64, 78.71) = 29.13, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .378 — closely matching the effect size found in Experiment 2 (η²ₚ = .394) and confirming that the paradigm continued to manipulate explicit SoA measurements successfully, albeit with a substantially smaller effect than in Experiment 1 (η²ₚ = .673). The post hoc comparison of Action Consistency confirmed that all three levels differed significantly from each other in the ratings they achieved (consistent vs. inconsistent: MD = 0.58, *t*(48) = 3.68, *p* = .002; consistent vs. control: MD = 1.20, *t*(48) = 6.44, *p* \< .001; inconsistent vs. control: MD = 0.62, *t*(48) = 5.13, *p* \< .001) — unlike in Experiment 2, where the consistent and inconsistent conditions did not differ significantly from one another. The simple main effect of Action Consistency, examined within each item, was again significant for all three (causation: *F*(2, 96) = 26.33, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .354; control: *F*(2, 96) = 25.86, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .350; prediction: *F*(2, 96) = 13.44, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .219), with prediction again showing the numerically smallest effect and therefore the least ability to discriminate between conditions.

Unlike in the previous two experiments, however, the main effect of Question type was not significant, *F*(1.87, 89.85) = 1.93, *p* = .153, η²ₚ = .039: collapsed across condition, the three questions no longer differed from one another in overall rating level. An exploratory simple main effect of Question type, examined within each level of Action Consistency, showed that the three items did not differ from one another within the consistent (*F*(2, 96) = 0.24, *p* = .788, η²ₚ = .005) or inconsistent conditions (*F*(2, 96) = 0.32, *p* = .725, η²ₚ = .007), but did differ significantly within the control condition (*F*(2, 96) = 11.82, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .198). This suggests greater convergence among the three items in the active conditions — where ratings became nearly indistinguishable from one another — while the control condition still showed more spread between items.

The Action Consistency × Question type interaction remained significant, *F*(2.41, 115.46) = 6.27, *p* = .001, η²ₚ = .116, though this was the smallest interaction effect size observed across the three experiments (Experiment 1: η²ₚ = .403; Experiment 2: η²ₚ = .192). The follow-up post hoc, conditioned on action consistency, showed no significant difference between any questions in either active condition, confirming the results above and supporting the claim that, with the larger movement required in this experiment, the questions no longer represented distinct aspects of explicit SoA but instead behaved as near-interchangeable measures within the active conditions. In the control condition, however, the predictive item again diverged from the other two, being rated significantly higher than both causation (*p* \< .001, *d* = −0.55) and controlled (*p* = .032, *d* = −0.39), while causation and controlled did not differ significantly from each other (*p* = .225, *d* = −0.16).
Conditional on question type, post hoc comparisons showed that all three questions were nonetheless able to distinguish between all three conditions — unlike in Experiment 2, where they failed to do so within the active conditions. The only exception here was the predictive item, which (as in Experiment 1) failed to differentiate the inconsistent from the control condition (*p* = .478) — it distinguished consistent from both of the other two, but not inconsistent from control.

In general, the results indicate that in this experiment, participants continued to discriminate between the three conditions through all three questions (unlike in Experiment 2, where the active conditions produced near-identical results across questions); however, results within each condition were noticeably more clustered, especially in the active conditions, with the predictive item remaining the most rigid — i.e., least discriminating — of the three. Taken together with Experiments 1 and 2, these results point to a consistent, monotonic decline across the three experiments in the questions' ability to differentiate from one another within a single active condition, alongside one unchanging exception: whenever participants were passive (the control condition), prediction was consistently the most highly endorsed item, in all three experiments without exception.

**Correlation**

Item scores were centered within-person in the same way as in Experiments 1 and 2 before entering the correlation analyses reported here. As in Experiment 2, the three items held together as a single coherent construct across every condition — every pairwise correlation between caused, controlled, and predicted reached significance in the consistent, inconsistent, and control conditions alike (rs = .28–.84, all *p* ≤ .048). 

The condition-specific composite was again correlated with the matching implicit RT effect (*d*). Consistent and control replicated the null pattern already seen in both previous experiments (consistent: r = .09, *p* = .523; control: r = .02, *p* = .878). The inconsistent condition, however, broke that pattern: the composite correlated significantly and negatively with *d* (r = −.29, *p* = .042). Participants whose explicit rating in the inconsistent condition sat above their own personal baseline (who responded with higher ratings to all questions) showed a more reversed gaze-cueing effect there, while those whose rating dropped relative to baseline showed a more typical, gaze-following pattern. A supplementary item-level check showed this relationship was not evenly distributed across items: controlled (r = −.41, p = .004) and predicted (r = −.30, p = .033) individually related to RT effect size d more strongly than the composite, while caused showed no relationship (r = −.03, p = .835).
