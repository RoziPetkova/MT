# Experiment 2

## Method

Experiment 2 employed the same within-subjects design as Experiment 1: two factors, gaze congruency (congruent, incongruent) and action consistency (control, consistent, inconsistent) (see Experiment 1 Method for the full factor definitions). The two experiments differed only in the response modality of the direction-selection response: instead of pressing a keyboard button, participants indicated their choice with a mouse click.

A fixation symbol preceded each trial: a cross ("+") or an asterisk ("\*"), one signaling a control trial and the other an action trial requiring a direction-selection response; the assignment of symbol to trial type was counterbalanced across participants, as in Experiment 1. At the onset of the action-trial symbol, the mouse cursor became visible at the center of the screen, just below the fixation symbol; it remained hidden throughout control trials. The face then appeared together with two bordered, unfilled rectangles positioned to its left and right — the same locations later occupied by the color target. Participants selected the gaze direction they wished to induce by moving the cursor into one of the two rectangles and clicking; the direction was scored according to which rectangle contained the cursor at the moment of the click. Clicks falling outside both rectangles were not registered and had no effect, such that only a click inside one of the two rectangles ended the response window.

Explicit sense of agency was again assessed at the end of the procedure using the same three self-report questions (predict / control / caused).

### Procedure

The trial structure otherwise followed Experiment 1 (fixation, face presentation, 210 ms gaze movement across seven frames, target-discrimination response window, inter-trial interval — see Experiment 1 Procedure for the full step-by-step timeline and Figure 1). The initiation time — the interval between face onset and the direction-selection response, later used as an ANCOVA covariate — was defined identically to Experiment 1: on control trials, in which no response was required, this interval was replaced by the corresponding face-display duration. The target-discrimination response remained a keyboard press using the a/d button pair; unlike Experiment 1, this assignment was not counterbalanced, since the direction-selection response no longer competed for the same keys.

The questionnaire phase (three Likert-scale questions, 7-point scale, 48 trials, 16 per condition) and the overall trial count (168 experimental trials, 28 per condition) were identical to Experiment 1.

### Participants

Sixty-one students took part in the experiment. One participant was excluded due to a technical problem during data collection and is not present in the dataset, leaving data from sixty participants (11 males, 49 females; M = 22.78 years, SD = 7.16). Participation was incentivized through ECTS credits tied to accuracy performance, and all participants provided written informed consent and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision.

## Results

**Data Exclusion and Final Sample**

Data were initially collected from 61 participants. One participant was excluded due to a technical problem during data collection and is not present in the dataset, leaving data from 60 participants. Four participants were excluded because their accuracy in the main experimental phase was below 85%, resulting in a sample of 56 participants, contributing a total of 9,408 trials.

Seven further participants were excluded based on predefined questionnaire data-quality criteria — invariant responding across items, defined as a dominant response exceeding 75% of all ratings combined with a condition-level response range of 0.5 or less (Meade & Craig, 2012); two additional participants were flagged by the same rule but retained because their response range exceeded 0.5, indicating they still discriminated between conditions. This resulted in a sample of 49 participants, contributing 8,232 trials.

From these trials, 3.88% (319 trials) were removed as error trials, leaving 7,913 trials. Trials with a reaction time below 200 ms were then excluded as physiologically implausible for this task (43 trials, 0.54%, spread across 14 participants), leaving 7,870 trials. A further 4.40% (346 trials) were excluded because their remaining reaction times deviated by more than 2 standard deviations from the mean, calculated separately for each participant and condition, leaving 7,524 trials.

One further participant was excluded because their overall mean reaction time exceeded the group mean by more than 2 standard deviations (cutoff: 708.7 ms, participant's mean: 715.3 ms).

The final analyses were therefore conducted on data from 48 participants.

**Questionary data**

**Descriptives**

As in Experiment 1, the three questions were answered on a seven-point Likert scale. Descriptive statistics for all questionnaire items across the three action-consistency conditions are presented in Table X.

Ratings followed the same ordering seen in Experiment 1 — consistent > inconsistent > control — but were compressed into a narrower and lower range throughout. In the consistent condition, mean ratings were highest for causation (M = 3.88, SD = 1.59), followed by prediction (M = 3.69, SD = 1.14) and control (M = 3.32, SD = 1.35). In the inconsistent condition, means were 3.76 (SD = 1.53) for causation, 3.40 (SD = 1.04) for prediction, and 3.07 (SD = 1.27) for control. Unlike Experiment 1, the control (passive) condition did not sit near the floor of the scale: mean ratings here ranged from 2.18 (SD = 1.07) for causation to 2.98 (SD = 1.25) for prediction.

This indicates that participants still distinguished action-generated from externally generated gaze movements, though less starkly than with a keypress response. Regarding question type, causation was again rated highest in both active conditions, though the margin over the other two items was smaller than in Experiment 1. In the control condition, prediction — not causation — received the highest ratings, replicating the reversal already observed in Experiment 1.

**Analyses**

As in Experiment 1, ratings were submitted to a 3 (Question type: caused, controlled, predicted) × 3 (Action Consistency: consistent, inconsistent, control) repeated-measures ANOVA, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied to all within-subject terms with more than one degree of freedom. Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated departures from normality in three of the nine cells, concentrated in the control condition; as before, the analysis is reported as planned given the aggregated dependent variable and the size of the effects below.

The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Action Consistency, *F*(1.73, 81.10) = 30.57, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .394: participants again reliably distinguished between the three conditions in how they answered the sense-of-agency questions, replicating the manipulation check from Experiment 1, though with a substantially smaller effect size than the η²ₚ = .673 found there. A significant main effect of Question type, *F*(1.64, 77.01) = 3.52, *p* = .043, η²ₚ = .070, showed that the three questions again differed in overall level of endorsement, though far more weakly than in Experiment 1 (η²ₚ = .343). These two effects were qualified by a significant Action Consistency × Question type interaction, *F*(2.18, 102.31) = 11.14, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .192 — again smaller than in Experiment 1 (η²ₚ = .403) — indicating that the discrimination between conditions was not equally strong across all three questions. As in Experiment 1, both directions of this interaction were examined in turn, each as its own Holm-corrected family of simple effects.

**Does action consistency affect the three questions differently?**

Simple main effects of Action Consistency were examined within each question type. Consistency significantly modulated ratings for all three questions — causation, *F*(2, 94) = 33.06, *p* \< .001; control, *F*(2, 94) = 23.40, *p* \< .001; prediction, *F*(2, 94) = 8.32, *p* \< .001 — replicating Experiment 1's finding that all three items are sensitive to the manipulation, again with causation showing the strongest sensitivity and prediction the weakest.

Holm-corrected pairwise comparisons revealed a pattern that departed from Experiment 1 in one respect: for all three questions, consistent and inconsistent trials no longer differed significantly from each other (causation, *p* = .309, *d* = 0.09; control, *p* = .083, *d* = 0.19; prediction, *p* = .099, *d* = 0.22), whereas in Experiment 1 this comparison had reached significance for causation and control. What remained intact was the separation between the active conditions and control: for causation, both consistent (*p* \< .001, *d* = 1.31) and inconsistent (*p* \< .001, *d* = 1.22) exceeded control; the control item showed the same pattern (consistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.78; inconsistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.59); and for prediction, both active conditions again exceeded control (consistent vs. control, *p* = .001, *d* = 0.55; inconsistent vs. control, *p* = .026, *d* = 0.32). In other words, participants in Experiment 2 registered whether they had made a choice at all, but not reliably whether that choice matched the outcome.

**Do the three questions differ from each other within the same condition?**

The reverse simple main effects — Question type within each level of Action Consistency — showed a different pattern from Experiment 1. Question type no longer significantly affected ratings in the consistent condition, *F*(2, 94) = 2.95, *p* = .057, and only marginally did so in the inconsistent condition, *F*(2, 94) = 4.23, *p* = .017; both are weaker than the strongly significant effects found for these same conditions in Experiment 1 (both *p* \< .001). In the control condition, question type remained clearly significant, *F*(2, 94) = 13.17, *p* \< .001, matching Experiment 1.

Because the simple effects in the consistent and inconsistent conditions did not reach significance, pairwise comparisons within those two conditions were not examined further. In the control condition, the same reversal seen in Experiment 1 replicated: prediction was rated higher than both causation (*p* \< .001, *d* = −0.62) and control (*p* = .003, *d* = −0.52), while causation and control did not differ from each other (*p* = .444, *d* = −0.09).

Overall, the questionnaire results in Experiment 2 replicate the two central findings from Experiment 1 — the action-consistency manipulation still shifts explicit sense-of-agency ratings, and the three questions are not interchangeable — but both effects are considerably attenuated, and the finer-grained distinction between consistent and inconsistent trials, present in Experiment 1, is no longer detectable on any of the three questions. **Reaction Time, Gaze Cueing, and Sense of Agency**

**Descriptive statistics**

Descriptive statistics were computed for reaction times (RTs) in the main experimental phase as a function of gaze congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) and action consistency (consistent, inconsistent, control). Means and standard deviations for each condition are presented in Table X.

The fastest responses were observed in the congruent-consistent condition (M = 495.40 ms, SD = 90.20), as in Experiment 1, whereas the slowest were again observed in the incongruent-control condition (M = 536.23 ms, SD = 106.52).

The mean congruency effect (incongruent minus congruent, computed per participant) was 27.92 ms (SD = 32.99) for consistent trials and 12.87 ms (SD = 33.47) for control trials — both in the expected direction, with incongruent trials slower than congruent trials. In the inconsistent condition, however, the mean difference was −10.76 ms (SD = 40.25), i.e., numerically faster responses on incongruent than congruent trials. As reported below, this pattern is not simply descriptive noise: the Gaze Congruency × Action Consistency interaction was significant, and the congruency effect differed reliably in size across the three consistency conditions.

Within gaze-congruent trials, RTs were fastest in the consistent condition (M = 495.40 ms, SD = 90.20), followed by inconsistent (M = 510.59 ms, SD = 87.42) and control (M = 523.36 ms, SD = 99.45). Within gaze-incongruent trials, the ordering differed: inconsistent trials were fastest (M = 499.82 ms, SD = 92.56), followed by consistent (M = 523.32 ms, SD = 94.91) and control (M = 536.23 ms, SD = 106.52).

**rANOVA**

To compare reaction times across experimental conditions, data were averaged by participant and entered into a repeated-measures ANOVA with gaze congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) and action consistency (consistent, inconsistent, control) as within-subject factors, Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied to all within-subject terms with more than one degree of freedom, and Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons.

There was a significant main effect of gaze congruency, *F*(1, 47) = 17.37, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .270, confirming a robust gaze-cueing effect: participants responded faster on congruent than on incongruent trials, replicating Experiment 1.

The main effect of action consistency was also significant, *F*(1.29, 60.56) = 13.28, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .220. Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons (collapsed across gaze congruency) showed that both consistent (M = 509.36 ms; *t*(47) = −3.29, *p* = .006, Hedges' *g* = −0.21) and inconsistent trials (M = 504.39 ms; *t*(47) = 4.22, *p* \< .001, *g* = 0.27) were faster than control trials (M = 529.90 ms), while consistent and inconsistent did not differ from each other (*p* = .220) — participants responded faster whenever an action was required, regardless of whether its outcome matched their choice.

Critically, the Gaze Congruency × Action Consistency interaction was significant, *F*(1.90, 89.38) = 12.17, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .206 — unlike Experiment 1, where the equivalent interaction did not reach significance. This indicates that the magnitude of the gaze-cueing effect genuinely differed across action-consistency conditions in Experiment 2.

To decompose this interaction, the simple congruency effect (incongruent minus congruent) was examined within each level of action consistency, Bonferroni-corrected as its own family of three comparisons (rather than pooled with the full set of pairwise cell comparisons, which tests a different, less specific question). The congruency effect was significant and in the expected direction within the consistent condition (27.92 ms, *SE* = 4.76, *t*(47) = 5.86, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.30) and within the control condition (12.87 ms, *SE* = 4.83, *t*(47) = 2.66, *p* = .011, *d* = 0.13), but in the inconsistent condition the effect was numerically reversed and did not reach significance (−10.76 ms, *SE* = 5.81, *t*(47) = 1.85, *p* = .070, *d* = 0.12). In other words, the standard gaze-cueing advantage for congruent trials held reliably when participants either made a matching choice or made no choice at all, but this advantage did not clearly hold — and numerically flipped — when the gaze moved opposite to the participant's chosen direction. This differential pattern across consistency conditions is what underlies the significant interaction. JASP's Simple Main Effects module (Gaze Congruency within each level of Action Consistency, using the omnibus model's pooled error term) confirms the same pattern: *F*(1, 47) = 34.38, *p* \< .001 for consistent; *F*(1, 47) = 7.10, *p* = .011 for control; *F*(1, 47) = 3.43, *p* = .070 for inconsistent.

Decomposing the same interaction from the other direction -- Action Consistency tested within each level of Gaze Congruency -- shows a clear asymmetry. Action Consistency has a significant effect on RT within congruent trials, *F*(2, 94) = 9.56, *p* \< .001, and a *larger* effect within incongruent trials, *F*(2, 94) = 16.06, *p* \< .001. The Action effect is present on both sides of the gaze manipulation, but is stronger specifically on incongruent trials -- consistent with the cell means above, where the reordering of conditions (inconsistent becoming numerically fastest) was specific to incongruent trials.

As in Experiment 1, a repeated-measures ANCOVA was conducted with mean initiation time (the interval between face onset and the direction-selection click, on action trials only) as a covariate, mean-centered, restricted to the two active conditions (consistent, inconsistent); the control condition was excluded because initiation time is not a meaningful behavioral measure there.

Gaze congruency was significant after controlling for initiation time, *F*(1, 46) = 7.80, *p* = .008, η²ₚ = .145. Action consistency was not, *F*(1, 46) = 3.03, *p* = .088, η²ₚ = .062, nor were either covariate interaction on its own (gaze × initiation time: *F*(1, 46) = 1.63, *p* = .208, η²ₚ = .034; action × initiation time: *F*(1, 46) = 0.58, *p* = .450, η²ₚ = .012).

Critically, the Gaze × Action interaction remained highly significant after controlling for initiation time, *F*(1, 46) = 21.21, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .316 -- if anything numerically larger than its plain-rANOVA counterpart above -- while the three-way interaction with initiation time was not significant, *F*(1, 46) = 0.03, *p* = .864, η²ₚ = .001. This indicates that, unlike in Experiment 1, the congruency-by-consistency interaction in Experiment 2 holds regardless of how quickly participants initiated their action: it is a robust effect on average, not one that depends on the covariate.

**Correlation**

Pearson correlations among the six RT cells and mean initiation time were computed. As in Experiment 1, all six RT conditions intercorrelated strongly and positively (*r* = .79–.95, all *p* \< .001), indicating stable individual differences in general response speed across conditions rather than any condition-specific pattern.

Unlike Experiment 1, initiation time correlated strongly and significantly with RT in every condition (*r* = .50–.67, all *p* \< .001; see the cross-experiment RT summary for the full comparison). This suggests that, for a mouse-click direction-selection response, initiation time shares much more variance with subsequent target-discrimination RT than it does for a simple keypress -- plausibly because a reach-and-click action draws on more of the same general psychomotor speed that also governs the later response, whereas a keypress decision is a fast, largely independent motor act.

**Bin Analysis**

As in Experiment 1, congruent- and incongruent-trial RTs were Vincentized separately within each action-consistency condition (sorted, split into 5 equal-size quantile bins per participant) and submitted to a 2 (Gaze) × 5 (bin) repeated-measures ANOVA, run separately per condition.

The Gaze main effect was significant in the consistent condition (F(1, 47) = 32.02, p \< .001, η²ₚ = .405) and the control condition (F(1, 47) = 6.93, p = .011, η²ₚ = .128), and marginal in the inconsistent condition (F(1, 47) = 3.19, p = .080, η²ₚ = .064). As in Experiment 1, the bin main effect was extremely large in all three conditions (RT necessarily increases across bins by construction), but **the Gaze × Bin interaction was not significant in any condition** (consistent: GG F(1.97, 92.51) = 0.68, p = .508; inconsistent: GG F(1.65, 77.32) = 0.33, p = .678; control: GG F(2.00, 93.87) = 0.23, p = .792).

Descriptively, the consistent condition's congruency gap declines gradually across the distribution (34, 28, 27, 26, 25 ms from bin 1 to bin 5) rather than being concentrated in either the fast or slow bins. The inconsistent condition shows a consistent *reversal* across every bin (−12, −8, −7, −11, −13 ms) -- the same reversal already found in the condition mean holds throughout the RT distribution, not just on average. The control condition shows a fairly flat gap across most of the distribution before narrowing in the slowest bin (13, 13, 15, 14, 8 ms), a somewhat different shape from Experiment 1's control pattern, but this is again not statistically reliable. As in Experiment 1, the bin analysis provides no statistical evidence that the congruency effect (or its reversal, in the inconsistent condition) is concentrated in a particular part of the RT distribution.
