- fluency - So, this paper argues that your sense of agency isn't only built after the fact, by comparing what you predicted would happen with what actually happened. That's the classic view. What they add is an earlier stage. Recent studies have revealed that a metacognitive signal about the fluency of action selection can prospectively inform our sense of agency. So, before you even act, how smoothly and easily you chose what to do already shapes how much control you feel over the outcome.

Their review looks at behavioral and brain-imaging data. They review several classes of behavioral and neuroimaging data suggesting that earlier processes, linked to fluency of action selection, prospectively contribute to sense of agency. So essentially, agency comes from two things combined. First, how easy the choice itself felt. Second, the more traditional matching between what you expected to happen and what actually happened afterward.

Now, how they operationalize fluency. Let me look that up specifically.Here's the operationalization, and it's quite clever.

They use subliminal priming of actions to manipulate the fluency of action selection, in a simple paradigm in which participants respond according to directional arrows and trigger the appearance of colored circles. Just before the target arrow appears, a masked, invisible prime arrow flashes briefly, pointing either the same direction, congruent, or the opposite direction, incongruent.

Action selection was easy when prime and target directions were congruent, but if the prime was incongruent with the target, this created a response conflict, which impaired action selection, leading to slower response times and more errors. So fluency is essentially operationalized as reaction time and error rate on the action itself, driven by this hidden congruency manipulation.

Then afterward, participants rate how much control they felt over which colored circle appeared. And the key finding is that incongruently primed actions led to a lower sense of agency over the action outcomes than congruently primed actions, even though the actual objective level of control participants had over the outcome never changed. So fluency was purely about how easy the action felt to select, not about actual performance or accuracy. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00320/full


If you actually want an answer to what increases sense of agency, that comes from a different strand of work — around voluntariness and choice. The greater the degree of voluntary action, the greater the sense of agency. And more specifically, from related work by Chambon and Haggard, selecting between alternative possible actions might itself generate a sense of agency — faced with two switches, deliberately choosing one produces a stronger sense of control than hesitating in confusion over which to pick.

So the more citable claim for your thesis isn't "operant beats underdetermined" — it's that clear, confident, low-conflict action selection produces stronger sense of agency than hesitant or conflicted selection. Which actually ties in nicely with that fluency-of-action-selection work we just went through.

The one about fluency of action selection specifically — the priming study with the arrows and colored circles — is Chambon, V., and Haggard, P. (2012), "Sense of control depends on fluency of action selection, not motor performance", published in the journal Cognition, volume 125.

The one about the choice between two switches, and voluntariness increasing sense of agency, comes from that same 2012 Chambon and Haggard paper as well — it's the illustrative example they use to introduce their broader argument before getting into the priming experiments.

And separately, the bigger review paper we started with tonight, the one about fluency prospectively informing sense of agency and reviewing behavioral and neuroimaging data, is Chambon, V., Sidarus, N., and Haggard, P.
