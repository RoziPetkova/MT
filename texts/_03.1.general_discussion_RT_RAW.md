# General Discussion — Reaction Time Findings Across Experiments

## Summary of Findings (RT)

Across all three experiments, a face's gaze consistently pulls attention toward its target: people respond faster to a target appearing on the side a face had just looked toward, regardless of whether they registered their own action with a keypress, a mouse click, or a touchscreen tap (all *p*s < .001). The action effect follows the same pattern: across all three experiments, people respond faster on trials where a direction-selection action was performed than on control trials where no action was taken. (note: should I add some interpretation here for the action effect?)

Regarding the central question - whether a person's own preceding action changes the size of this gaze effect - the results slightly vary across the three experiments.

In Experiment 1, the gaze effect and the action effect operated independently: both showed individual main effects, but no significant interaction between them, *F*(1.98, 79.38) = 1.09, *p* = .341. A keypress response did not appear substantial enough to become bound to the outcome that followed it. Although congruent trials were numerically faster than incongruent ones across all action conditions, simple-effects analysis showed no significant GC effect in the inconsistent action condition.
Once the action involved a larger, more deliberate movement — a mouse click for direction selection in place of a keypress (Experiment 2) — the gaze effect and the action effect began to interact, *F*(1.90, 89.38) = 12.17, *p* < .001. The GC effect remained intact when the action's outcome matched the participant's selection, or when no action was taken. When the action's outcome contradicted the selection, however, the GC effect was not just reduced — it reversed direction. Although the difference did not reach significance (*p* = .070), incongruent trials were numerically faster than congruent ones in the inconsistent condition. In other words, people reacted faster to targets that appeared on the side they had selected than on the side cued by the external gaze cue. In Experiment 3 (where selection was made via touchscreen), the same pattern recurred. The interaction remained significant, *F*(1.71, 82.23) = 13.43, *p* < .001, and the reversal within the inconsistent condition approached significance on its own, *p* = .056 (and even reached significance once individual differences in initiation time were controlled for). This suggests that the outcome of one's own action may interfere with, rather than simply fail to add to, the effect of someone else's gaze — especially when there was a mismatch between the predicted/desired and actual outcome.
A further question was whether the modality of the direction-selection movement could add to the divergence in the gaze-cueing effect. A cross-experiment comparison was conducted to test this. On raw response times, this produced a three-way interaction that fell just short of conventional significance, *p* = .061. Furthermore, a comparison of the standardized gaze-cueing effect (Cohen's *d*) showed a significant three-way interaction, suggesting that movement modality does plausibly play a role, compounding the influence of action-outcome consistency on gaze cueing. Further exploration showed that this difference was most clearly seen between the button-press and touchscreen-tap studies, specifically in the inconsistent condition, with the mouse-click study positioned between the two without being clearly distinguishable from either.

Taken together, the results suggest that what mattered was action-outcome consistency — specifically, when the action's outcome violated the participant's expectation, which resulted in an unstable gaze-cueing effect. Once movement modality was taken into account, this instability went further still: the GC effect did not merely disappear, but reversed.
