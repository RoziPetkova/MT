# General Discussion — Reaction Time Findings Across Experiments

## Summary of Findings (RT)

Across all three experiments, a face's gaze pulls attention where it looks: people consistently respond faster to a target appearing on the side a face just glanced toward than on the opposite side. This basic gaze-cueing effect shows up reliably no matter how a person in the study registered their directional choices — pressing a key, clicking a mouse, or tapping a touchscreen. Separately, simply having made an active choice speeds people up too: across all three experiments, people respond faster overall on trials where they chose something — whether or not it matched what happened — than on trials where the gaze direction was decided for them. Neither of these two basic effects depends on how people made their directioal choice (movement modality).

What is not automatic is whether a person's own preceding choice changes the size of the gaze effect. When people simply pressed a key to choose a direction, the gaze boost stayed about the same size regardless of what happened afterward — whether the face's gaze matched their choice, went the opposite way, or had nothing to do with them. Their own choice and the face's gaze behaved, for practical purposes, like two separate things. - ??

That stops being true once the choosing action itself becomes more physical. With a mouse click or a touchscreen tap, the gaze effect holds up exactly as expected whenever the choice was either fulfilled (the face looked where they'd picked) or beside the point (no choice was made at all) — people are still faster when gaze points at the target. But when the choice was directly contradicted — they picked one direction and the face looked the other way — that boost does not simply disappear. People trend toward being *slower*, not faster, on the very trials where gaze points straight at the target: as if being wrong about their own choice actively works against the normal pull of someone else's gaze, rather than just cancelling it out. This disruption gets stronger the more the choosing action resembles an actual movement toward a location — barely present with a keypress, clearly present with a mouse click, and more pronounced still with a touchscreen tap.

One wrinkle is worth explaining plainly, because it shapes how the cross-experiment result is reported: on raw response times alone, this pattern sits right at the edge of conventional statistical significance — visible, but not decisive. That is not because the effect is shaky. A keypress, a mouse click, and a touchscreen tap do not necessarily produce the same amount of natural moment-to-moment variability in how fast someone responds, and comparing raw milliseconds across the three experiments implicitly assumes they do. Correcting for that — in two independent ways, which agree with each other — turns this into a clean, reliable result: a self-generated mismatch between what someone meant to happen and what actually happened measurably disrupts the ordinary boost from following someone's gaze, and disrupts it more, the more physically involved the choosing action is. The specific comparison this holds up to statistically is between the keypress study and the touchscreen study; the mouse study sits in between the two without being cleanly distinguishable from either one on its own.

## 2×3 rANOVA and main effects (Gaze × Action)

Each experiment's reaction-time data was submitted to a 2 (Gaze Congruency: congruent, incongruent) × 3 (Action Consistency: consistent, inconsistent, control) repeated-measures ANOVA. This tests three things at once: whether the gaze cueing effect (GCE) is present, whether action-outcome consistency (AOC) itself affects RT, and whether the two influence each other through an interaction.

| Effect | Experiment 1 (keypress) | Experiment 2 (mouse) | Experiment 3 (touch) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaze | F(1,40)=14.25, p<.001, η²ₚ=.263 | F(1,47)=17.37, p<.001, η²ₚ=.270 | F(1,48)=13.72, p<.001, η²ₚ=.222 |
| Action | F(1.45,58.15)=5.38, p=.014, η²ₚ=.119 | F(1.29,60.56)=13.28, p<.001, η²ₚ=.220 | F(1.49,71.40)=3.97, p=.034, η²ₚ=.076 |
| Gaze × Action | F(1.98,79.38)=1.09, p=.341 (n.s.) | F(1.90,89.38)=12.17, p<.001, η²ₚ=.206 | F(1.71,82.23)=13.43, p<.001, η²ₚ=.219 |

The gaze cueing effect and the action-consistency effect are both present in all three experiments. Whether the two interact differs by experiment: with a keypress response (Experiment 1), the size of the GCE does not depend on which action condition a trial belongs to; with a mouse click (Experiment 2) or a touchscreen tap (Experiment 3), it does — the GCE's size and the action-consistency manipulation are not independent.

### Where the GCE holds: simple main effects of Gaze within each Action condition

To localize the interaction, the simple gaze effect was tested separately within each action-consistency condition.

| Condition | Experiment 1 | Experiment 2 | Experiment 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consistent | F(1,40)=12.21, p=.001 | F(1,47)=34.38, p<.001 | F(1,48)=28.28, p<.001 |
| Control | F(1,40)=5.49, p=.024 | F(1,47)=7.10, p=.011 | F(1,48)=17.72, p<.001 |
| Inconsistent | F(1,40)=0.86, p=.360 (n.s.) | F(1,47)=3.43, p=.070 (n.s.) | F(1,48)=3.83, p=.056 (n.s.) |

The GCE is present on congruent trials whenever the action matched the outcome (consistent) or when no action was taken at all (control), in all three experiments. It is not reliable when the action's outcome did not match what was chosen (inconsistent) — in Experiment 1 this is simply a weaker, still-positive effect; in Experiments 2 and 3 the effect is not just weaker but numerically reversed (participants trend slower, not faster, on congruent trials), though this reversal does not reach significance on its own in either experiment.

### Where AOC matters: simple main effects of Action within each Gaze level

The interaction can equally be decomposed the other way — testing whether action consistency affects RT differently depending on whether gaze was congruent or incongruent.

| Gaze level | Experiment 1 | Experiment 2 | Experiment 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Congruent | F(2,80)=3.77, p=.027 | F(2,94)=9.56, p<.001 | F(2,96)=4.02, p=.021 |
| Incongruent | F(2,80)=4.79, p=.011 | F(2,94)=16.06, p<.001 | F(2,96)=11.41, p<.001 |

In Experiment 1, action consistency affects RT about equally regardless of gaze congruency — no asymmetry. In Experiments 2 and 3, action consistency affects RT on both sides of the gaze manipulation, but the effect is clearly stronger on incongruent trials, and this asymmetry is larger in Experiment 3 than in Experiment 2.

### Synthesis

All three experiments show a real GCE and a real AOC effect on RT. What differs is whether the two show up as a fixed, unconditional interaction. In Experiment 1, no such interaction is detected (p=.341) — though as the ANCOVA below shows, this does not mean the two effects are simply independent; the interaction reappears once initiation time is taken into account. In Experiments 2 and 3, the interaction is significant on its own, and in both experiments it is driven by the same place — the inconsistent condition, where the standard gaze-cueing advantage is not reliable and trends toward reversal. Looked at from the other direction, the action-consistency effect itself is not uniform across gaze levels in either Experiment 2 or Experiment 3: it is stronger specifically on incongruent trials in both. Together this describes a single pattern, not two separate ones — the inconsistent condition is where action and gaze stop being independent. A keypress response (Experiment 1) does not show this coupling as a fixed effect; a mouse click and a touchscreen tap (Experiments 2 and 3) both do. Whether this reflects a genuine step change between response types, or a smoother gradient that this decomposition alone can't resolve, is addressed directly by the formal cross-experiment comparison later in this chapter.
