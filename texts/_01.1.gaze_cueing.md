## Part I — Social Attention and Gaze Cuing

Social interaction is a key part of social functioning. Some of the most fundamental human activities take place in interaction with others: people hold conversations, have confrontations, form bonds and communicate emotions face to face (Hamilton & Holler, 2023). Interaction with others is part of everyday life, helps us navigate the external environment and supports some core human abilities; even the human faculty to speak is thought to have evolved for communicating with others (Kuhlen & Abdel Rahman, 2023). Social interaction is therefore not just something people do, but also something that shapes how we think: behaviour, brain processes and cognition operate differently when people are in an interaction with another person (Hamilton & Holler, 2023), and even a short period of social interaction can improve subsequent cognitive performance (Ybarra et al., 2008).

To function in such an environment, however, people must constantly decide what to attend to. The environment is crowded with stimuli that may need further scrutiny, and attending to what is relevant is a prerequisite for effective functioning (Nummenmaa & Hietanen, 2009). Attention is the mechanism that performs this selection, giving preferential processing to some inputs over others (Frischen et al., 2007). Social information occupies a privileged position in this selection: social interactions have priority access in attention and working memory (McMahon & Isik, 2023), and actions that are part of a social interaction gain preferential access to visual awareness over non-interactive ones (Su et al., 2016). To guide this selection, people also rely on cues provided by others, many of which are nonverbal, such as pointing gestures, head orientation and gaze direction, and which serve to orient attention to important events in the world (Nummenmaa & Hietanen, 2009). Following such cues allows us to share another person's focus of attention, the basis of joint attention (Tomasello et al., 2005), to make inferences about their internal states (Baron-Cohen, 1995) and, therefore, to filter what is important to others. One of the most important of these cues is gaze: people are drawn to the eyes of others (Batki et al., 2000), especially when the gaze is directed at them (Farroni et al., 2002; Senju & Johnson, 2009b), and they tend to follow it when it shifts (Emery, 2000; Friesen & Kingstone, 1998).

The eyes are among the most informative features of the face, so it is not surprising that their importance extends well beyond our own species and is shared with our closest relatives. Other primates also follow the gaze of conspecifics, adjusting their attention to where others are looking (Emery, 2000; Shepherd et al., 2006). In a gaze-cueing task adapted for macaques, for example, monkeys oriented toward targets appearing where a pictured conspecific was looking, much as humans do, although the strength of this response depended on the social status of the monkey in the picture (Shepherd et al., 2006). This shared capacity suggests a common evolutionary origin that predates modern humans: following the gaze of others appears to be an ancient mechanism for coordinating attention and action within social groups.

Although gaze following is not limited to humans, there are significant differences in how it operates across species. When following the gaze of a human experimenter, for example, great apes relied mainly on the direction of the head, whereas human infants relied almost exclusively on the direction of the eyes (Tomasello et al., 2007). One reason for this difference may lie in the morphology of the eye itself. Unlike that of other primates, the human eye has a white, widely visible sclera surrounding a relatively small pupil and iris (Kobayashi & Kohshima, 1997; Tomasello et al., 2007). Far from being a mere anatomical detail, this configuration has a functional consequence: it makes gaze direction extremely easy to detect (Kobayashi & Kohshima, 1997) and allows the rapid and efficient extraction of social information through robust processing mechanisms (Langton et al., 2000). In natural social interactions, it enables individuals to read the attentional focus of others even at a distance, in peripheral vision, or during brief glances, a capacity that is far more limited in species whose sclera is less visible (Kobayashi & Kohshima, 1997).

The specific human structure of the eye has additional consequences. For example, a classic psychophysical study by Gibson and Pick (1963) revealed an extraordinary sensitivity to subtle changes in gaze angle: when the looker’s head faced the observer, the threshold at which observers accepted a truly deviated gaze as direct was just 2.8°. Such fine-grained sensitivity is thought to reflect the high degree of social coordination required in human groups (Langton et al., 2000). Humans are also remarkably good at sensing when they are being watched: direct gaze is detected faster and more accurately than averted gaze, even in peripheral vision and at very brief exposures (Senju & Johnson, 2009b).

From an evolutionary perspective, two major explanations have been proposed for this profound human sensitivity to the eyes and gaze (see the discussion in Langton et al., 2000). According to the first, evolutionary pressures have dedicated specific neural resources to capturing information from the gaze of others, in the form of specialized brain circuits for gaze processing, initially highlighted by Perrett and colleagues (Perrett et al., 1990, for animals; Haxby et al., 2000, for humans; Puce & Perrett, 2013). Within this network, the superior temporal sulcus (STS) has been repeatedly implicated in coding gaze direction and in distinguishing direct from averted gaze (Hoffman & Haxby, 2000; Pelphrey et al., 2003), while the rapid detection of eye contact is thought to be supported by a fast subcortical pathway (Senju & Johnson, 2009b). The second explanation focuses on the structure of the eye itself, which seems to have evolved to be easily detected by the human visual system, turning gaze into a social signal that is impossible to ignore. On this view, the human eye has been shaped by natural selection not only for seeing, but also for being seen, a proposal that has received considerable support in comparative studies of primate visual morphology (Kobayashi & Kohshima, 1997; see also Langton et al., 2000).

Developmental research also supports the evolutionary view of the importance of the eyes, showing that sensitivity to them emerges remarkably early. From the first days of life, newborns show a preference for face-like stimuli and preferentially orient toward faces (Farroni et al., 2002; Johnson et al., 2015), and at the center of this attention are the eyes. Newborns already prefer faces with open eyes (Batki et al., 2000) and faces with direct rather than averted gaze (Farroni et al., 2002; Senju & Johnson, 2009b), suggesting that eye-contact detection is present from birth. By 3–4 months of age, infants’ attention to objects is already facilitated by the gaze direction of an adult (Reid & Striano, 2005).

The role of gaze in social cognition has also been extensively studied. Baron-Cohen (1995) proposed that the ability to detect and follow gaze is supported by a specialized cognitive module, the Eye Direction Detector (EDD), which functions as a precursor to theory of mind. In his model, the EDD is one of several neurocognitive modules that build on one another: it detects the presence of eyes, computes gaze direction, and infers whether the gaze is directed toward the self or toward another object. Operating automatically and at a low level of processing, it provides the raw material for more sophisticated social-cognitive processes. The EDD is hypothesized to be innate and to mature very early in development, consistent with the finding that newborns are already sensitive to direct eye contact (Farroni et al., 2002). Gaze following is therefore not merely an attentional orienting mechanism, but a gateway to complex social cognition: detecting gaze direction enables the attribution of intentions and mental states to others, processes that underlie theory of mind (Baron-Cohen, 1995; Baron-Cohen et al., 1997). Notably, disturbances in this early gaze-processing module have been proposed as a core feature of autism spectrum disorder, where both direct gaze detection and joint attention are atypical from early childhood (Baron-Cohen, 1995; Senju & Johnson, 2009a).

Beyond the mere detection of gaze direction, direct eye contact, i.e. the actual meeting of gazes, constitutes a social signal with a special status: it signals an intention to interact, captures attention faster, holds it longer, and activates neural networks involved in social cognition (Senju & Johnson, 2009b). Once mutual attention is established, a subsequent shift in the other person’s gaze acquires a deictic function: like a pointing gesture, it indicates an object or location without naming it, inviting us to look there too (Pfeiffer et al., 2013). The resulting tendency to shift one’s own attention in the direction of another person’s gaze is known as gaze cueing (GC). It was first reported at the end of the 20th century, when three research groups independently adapted a classic attention paradigm, replacing a symbolic cue, such as an arrow, with a face looking to the left or to the right. Participants responded faster to targets that appeared where the face was looking than to targets that appeared on the opposite side, even when the gaze did not predict the target’s location (Driver et al., 1999; Friesen & Kingstone, 1998; Langton & Bruce, 1999). This response advantage for gazed-at locations is known as the gaze-cueing effect (GCE). Since these first studies, gaze cueing has become one of the most studied phenomena in social attention (McKay et al., 2021), and it is widely considered a fundamental mechanism through which we orient toward what others attend to.

Taken together, comparative, neural and developmental findings converge on the same conclusion: the eyes are a social signal of special importance, with deep evolutionary roots and an early developmental onset, and the foundation on which the more complex cognitive capacities like joint attention and theory of mind are built. The gaze-cueing paradigm reproduces the sequence described above, eye contact followed by a shift of gaze, in a simplified and controlled form, and therefore offers a privileged experimental window into how gaze direction orients our attention and makes the factors that modulate this orienting accessible to empirical investigation. In the present study, we use this paradigm to examine a possible modulator factor – the sense of agency, by letting participants themselves trigger the shift in the face’s gaze.

The gaze-cueing paradigm, which has become fundamental to the study of gaze following, originated from the classical Posner attentional orienting task (Posner, 1980). In the Posner task, a central or peripheral cue is presented, often an arrow, indicating the probable location of an upcoming target. The difference in reaction times is then calculated between valid trials, in which the cue correctly indicated the location of the upcoming target, and invalid trials, in which the cue indicated the wrong location. This difference represents the attentional benefit conferred by cue-based orienting. In the late 1990s, three research groups independently adapted this paradigm, replacing the arrow with a social cue: a human face whose gaze was directed to the left or to the right (Driver et al., 1999; Friesen & Kingstone, 1998; Langton & Bruce, 1999). Together, these studies showed that the eyes, as a social cue, can guide attentional orienting, whether together with the orientation of the head (Langton & Bruce, 1999), alone in a schematic face (Friesen & Kingstone, 1998), or alone in a realistic face whose head remained still (Driver et al., 1999). In all three cases, orienting occurred even though the cue did not predict the target location, that is, independently of its strategic utility. 

The seemingly minor modification of replacing an arrow with a gaze thus opened an entire field of research, demonstrating that attentional orienting is not governed exclusively by arbitrary symbols, but also by social signals with deep ecological relevance. At the same time, the early research showed that the two categories of cues differ in a fundamental respect: while symbolic cues (arrows) orient attention predominantly when they are predictive, gaze produces a robust effect even when the participant knows that its direction does not predict the target location (Friesen & Kingstone, 1998, but see Frischen et al., 2007, for later evidence of cueing by non-predictive arrows). This resistance to non-predictiveness is one of the defining features of the gaze-cueing effect and suggests that gaze engages attentional mechanisms partially distinct from those involved in symbolic cues, as later studies demonstrated (Frischen et al., 2007; Hietanen et al., 2006). Gaze, therefore, is not merely a more “ecological” arrow, but a special social cue.

To better understand the nature of GCE and the mechanisms that govern it, it is useful to first detail the typical structure of a gaze-cueing experiment. This same structure underlies the three experiments on which the present work is based and both will be described simultaneously here. It is as follows: the participant is seated in front of a screen and fixates a central point. In the center of the screen, a human face (schematic or photographic) appears. The face can be displayed directly with averted gaze – in the static versions of the paradigm or with gaze initially directed straight ahead that later shifts to the left or right – in the dynamic version. Both static faces (photographs with averted gaze) and dynamic faces (animated sequences in which the eyes move) produce the cueing effect. Findings on whether the two versions differ in the magnitude of the effect are mixed, with dynamic gaze shifts producing a larger effect in some studies under specific conditions (Bayless et al., 2011; Dalmaso et al., 2020; Frischen et al., 2007).

After a short interval (usually between 300 and 1000 ms), the face’s gaze shifts to the left or to the right. At a variable interval (stimulus onset asynchrony, SOA)—that is, the interval between the onset of the cue and the onset of the target—a peripheral target appears. This target can vary, but in the case of the present experiments, it is a square of a certain color. The target appears either in the direction indicated by the gaze (congruent trial) or in the opposite direction (incongruent trial). The participant’s task is to execute a task – usually detect, categorize or discriminate the target as quickly as possible.

The gaze-cueing effect (Gaze Cueing Effect, GCE) is operationally defined as the difference between the mean reaction times recorded in incongruent trials and those recorded in congruent trials: GCE = RT(incongruent) – RT(congruent), expressed in milliseconds. A positive GCE indicates that participants responded faster to targets appearing in the direction of gaze, confirming that the social cue facilitated attentional orienting. The magnitude of GCE typically lies in the range of about 10–20 ms (e.g., Bayliss et al., 2005; Bayliss & Tipper, 2006; Wiese et al., 2012), although it can be considerably larger under demanding task conditions (Gregory & Jackson, 2021), and it has been replicated in hundreds of studies, being considered one of the most robust phenomena in the social attention literature (Frischen et al., 2007; McKay et al., 2021).

One of the particularly relevant aspects of the gaze-cueing effect is its profoundly automatic nature. Eloquent evidence in this regard comes from studies that manipulated the interval between cue and target (SOA). For example, one of the first experimental investigations of this effect, Friesen and Kingstone (1998), showed that although the effect is significant at SOAs of 105, 300, and 600 ms, it disappears completely at an SOA of 1005 ms. The fact that the effect is present at very short SOAs but disappears after approximately one second corresponds to the typical temporal profile of exogenous orienting: rapid in onset but transient, dissipating in less than a second (Müller & Rabbitt, 1989). Exogenous orienting refers to the orienting of attention triggered involuntarily by an external stimulus, independent of the observer's intentions. A familiar example is a sudden flash of light in the periphery, which captures attention automatically, even before we become consciously aware of it. In contrast, endogenous orienting is slower, voluntary, guided by symbolic cues and by the observer's expectations (Posner, 1980).

Another important aspect of the GCE was also demonstrated by Friesen and Kingstone (1998). They showed a marked asymmetry between benefits and costs: the effect proved to be purely facilitatory, without reducing the possibility to allocate attention to uncued locations. Reaction times were faster in trials in which the gaze indicated the correct location compared to a neutral condition, without any significant slowing in trials in which the gaze indicated the wrong location, a pattern later replicated by other studies (Hietanen & Leppänen, 2003; Mathews et al., 2003). This contrasts with classic spatial cueing studies, in which cues typically produce both a benefit for validly cued locations and a cost for invalidly cued ones (Posner, 1980; Frischen et al., 2007). In other words, gaze produces an attentional benefit without a corresponding cost: attention is drawn toward the gazed-at location, but without being withdrawn from the opposite side, so that targets appearing there are processed as efficiently as in the neutral condition. This combination of automaticity and a purely facilitatory profile makes the gaze-cueing effect a particularly interesting case for investigating whether higher-order factors, such as the sense of agency, can modulate its expression.

Moreover, the effect persists under conditions that, theoretically, should abolish it if it relies on strategic, controlled processes. For example, gaze-triggered orienting remains robust even when the cue is presented very briefly and then masked, preventing elaborate conscious processing (Sato et al., 2007). It is worth noting that Sato and colleagues (2007) demonstrated the same automatic orienting effect for cues provided by head direction, suggesting that not only the eyes but also head posture can trigger attentional shifts without awareness. The effect is also maintained when gaze direction does not predict the target location better than chance (Galfano et al., 2012) and even when the cue is counter predictive, that is, when it systematically indicates the opposite location of the target (Kuhn & Kingstone, 2009; Tipples, 2008). Additionally, the effect becomes even stronger when attentional resources are depleted by perceptual distraction and increased task demands, making nonpredictive cues harder to ignore (Gregory & Jackson, 2021). This remarkable robustness is consistent with the idea that gaze-triggered orienting is a fundamental mechanism, deeply rooted in human cognitive architecture. 
Taken together, these findings suggest that the gaze-cueing effect shares core features with exogenous orienting, such as its rapid onset and its resistance to non-predictiveness (Friesen & Kingstone, 1998), while retaining social specificity in other respects, such as benefits without costs (Friesen & Kingstone, 1998), a longer time course, and partly distinct neural substrates (Hietanen et al., 2006; McKay et al., 2021).


From the evidence reviewed so far, gaze cueing emerges as a core attentional mechanism: rapid, largely automatic and hard to modify. At the same time, however, a growing body of research has shown that the magnitude of this effect is not fixed: it is modulated by a variety of social and cognitive factors, including the characteristics of the cueing face, the observer's characteristics, and the social-relational context in which the cue is embedded (for reviews, see Dalmaso et al., 2020; McKay et al., 2021). The coexistence of a strong automatic component with systematic modulability is not contradictory; rather, it suggests that gaze-triggered orienting is best conceptualized as a rapid, reflexive process that is nonetheless permeable to higher-order influences. This is precisely what motivates the present study: if the gaze-cueing effect can be modulated by factors such as the emotional expression of the face or the observer's social group membership, then it is plausible that an internal state generated by one's own action — the sense of agency — might also shape this fundamental social-attentional process.


Moreover, recent computational models of evidence accumulation have hypothesized that the speeding of responses to gazed-at targets might reflect an increase in the efficiency of information processing at the indicated location, rather than a simple facilitation of attentional orienting (Alister et al., 2024; Lassalle & Itier, 2015; Reid & Striano, 2005). This distinction is relevant for the present study: if the sense of agency over the gaze modifies not only attentional orienting but also the depth of information processing at the indicated location, then the modulation of the gaze-cueing effect by agency might reflect a strategic redistribution of cognitive resources, rather than merely a simple inhibition of an automatic reflex.

## Part II — What Modulates Gaze Cuing?


As mentioned, although robust and largely automatic, the gaze-cueing effect is not insensitive to context. A vast literature has demonstrated that its magnitude is modulated by a multitude of factors (for reviews, see Dalmaso et al., 2020; McKay et al., 2021). These factors can be grouped into three broad categories: characteristics of the face providing the cue, such as its emotional expression or perceived social status; characteristics of the relationship between the observer and the face, such as familiarity, group membership or a shared history of interaction; and characteristics of the observer, ranging from stable traits such as gender, age or anxiety to temporary internal states. In the following paragraphs, each category is examined in turn.

### Characteristics of the cueing face

The influence of emotional expressions on the gaze-cueing effect has received considerable attention, but the findings are notably mixed. On the one hand, several studies have reported that faces expressing negative emotions, particularly fear, amplify the gaze-cueing effect. For example, Bayless et al. (2011) demonstrated that fearful and surprised faces produce a larger cueing effect than happy or angry faces, and that this enhancement disappeared when only the eye region of the faces was presented, even though the eyes alone preserved the perceptual feature thought to drive it (eye widening). This finding is important because it shows that the emotional modulation of the gaze-cueing effect is not merely an artifact of low-level perceptual differences between emotional and neutral faces, but reflects a genuine contribution of emotional significance to attentional orienting. On the other hand, a considerable number of studies have found no reliable modulation of the gaze-cueing effect by emotional expression (e.g., Hietanen & Leppänen, 2003; for a review, see Dalmaso et al., 2020).

Theoretically, the enhancement of the gaze-cueing effect by fearful expressions is thought to reflect the adaptive significance of threat detection, in line with the attentional vigilance to threatening faces documented in other paradigms (Bradley et al., 2000; Eastwood et al., 2001; Fox et al., 2000; Mogg & Bradley, 1999). If another person’s fear signals the presence of a threat in the environment, attending to the gazed-at location can be beneficial for personal safety. Accordingly, Mathews et al. (2003) first reported a greater gaze orienting effect for fearful compared to neutral faces in participants with high trait anxiety, who have a lower threshold for threat processing, a finding that has since been replicated (Fox et al., 2007; Holmes et al., 2006; Tipples, 2006). Not all threatening expressions, however, produce the same effect. Unlike fear, which signals a threat located elsewhere, anger signals a direct social confrontation in which the angry person is themselves the source of threat. An angry face with averted gaze should therefore not enhance orienting toward the gazed-at location in the same way as a fearful face. Consistent with this, Fox et al. (2007) found a larger gaze orienting effect for fearful than for angry faces in anxious individuals, while angry faces with direct gaze slowed responses more than any other emotion, presumably because they signal an immediate threat and capture attentional resources that would otherwise be available for target processing. Because emotional expression is one of the most studied facial modulators, the present study used a neutral face as the cueing stimulus, so that emotional content would not confound the effects of the sense of agency.

Beyond emotional expression, the social meaning conveyed by the face also modulates the gaze-cueing effect. One of the most intensively investigated facial cues in this respect is dominance, to which, as noted earlier, even macaques are sensitive (Shepherd et al., 2006). In humans, Jones et al. (2010) manipulated face shape using computer-graphic techniques (Tiddeman et al., 2001) to create masculinized and feminized versions of the same faces. Both male and female observers showed larger gaze-cueing effects for masculinized faces. This is important because masculinized faces are consistently perceived as more dominant than feminized ones (Perrett et al., 1998; Watkins et al., 2010), and masculine facial features have been linked to objective indicators of dominance, such as physical strength (Fink et al., 2007) and scores on social dominance questionnaires (Quist et al., 2011). Interestingly, the effect was observed not only when observers judged faces of the opposite sex, but also when women viewed other women’s faces. This is somewhat surprising given the long-standing assumption that dominance is less relevant for female than for male social behavior (Buunk et al., 2008, as cited in Jones et al., 2011), and suggests that, at the level of automatic orienting, dominance information is processed irrespective of the observer’s gender. Moreover, the effect decreased as stimulus onset asynchrony increased, supporting the idea that it arises from fast, involuntary processes rather than from deliberative social judgments.

The influence of perceived social status, which is conceptually related to dominance, provides further evidence that the gaze-cueing effect is shaped by socially relevant knowledge about the cueing face. In a study by Dalmaso et al. (2012), participants were presented with faces that had been previously associated with either high or low social status through fictional curricula vitae. The results showed that faces perceived as high-status elicited a larger gaze-cueing effect than low-status faces, even though the faces themselves were physically identical across conditions. This finding demonstrates that the gaze-cueing effect is not determined solely by the physical properties of the face but is flexibly shaped by socially relevant knowledge about the person. Such flexibility is a hallmark of higher-order social cognition operating on top of a fundamental reflexive mechanism.

A second group of modulators concerns not the face itself, but its relationship with the observer. Familiarity is one such factor: Deaner et al. (2007) found that familiar faces produced a larger cueing effect than unfamiliar faces, although only in female participants. Group membership has a similar influence. Faces belonging to the observer’s own racial group elicit a larger gaze-cueing effect than faces from a different racial group (Pavan et al., 2011), and the gaze of politicians from the observer’s own political group captures attention more strongly than the gaze of politicians from the opposing group (Liuzza et al., 2011). Finally, the role of trust has also been investigated, although with mixed results. Süßenbach and Schönbrodt (2014) found a larger gaze-cueing effect for faces described as belonging to trustworthy individuals, but not in highly anxious participants. Bayliss and Tipper (2006) showed the opposite direction of this relationship: faces whose gaze reliably predicted the target location were later judged as more trustworthy. Other studies, in contrast, found no modulation of the gaze-cueing effect by trustworthiness (for a review, see Dalmaso et al., 2020).


Together, these studies show that the gaze-cueing effect is not blind to who the cueing face is to the observer: it is modulated by the perceived relevance and similarity of the face, by shared group membership and, at least in some conditions, by its perceived trustworthiness. Social categorization and social knowledge about the person can thus penetrate even relatively automatic components of social attention, indicating that gaze cueing is influenced by higher-order social concepts. If the gaze-cueing effect is sensitive to who the face is to the observer, it may also be sensitive to whether the observer’s own action caused the face’s gaze shift.

### Characteristics of the observer

The third group of modulators concerns the observer, ranging from relatively stable features, such as gender, age and personality traits, to temporary internal states. This group is of particular interest for the present work, since it includes the internal states to which the sense of agency belongs.

Among stable characteristics, one of the most consistently reported is gender. Bayliss et al. (2005) found that women tend to exhibit a larger gaze-cueing effect than men, a result subsequently replicated by several independent research groups (e.g., Alwall et al., 2010; for a review, see Dalmaso et al., 2020). This finding suggests that gender differences in social attention may reflect more general differences in the sensitivity to social signals: women are often considered to be more socially oriented than men (Baron-Cohen, 2003; Geary, 2006) and, on average, show a stronger automatic orienting response to gaze.

Age is another observer-related factor that shapes the gaze-cueing effect. Although sensitivity to gaze is present from the first days of life, the effect tends to decline in older age: older adults show a smaller gaze-cueing effect than younger adults (Slessor et al., 2008; Slessor et al., 2010, as cited in Dalmaso et al., 2020) and are less influenced by the gaze of a distractor face in their eye movements (Kuhn et al., 2015). Interestingly, younger adults show a larger effect for faces of their own age, an own-age bias that is absent in older adults (Slessor et al., 2010, as cited in Dalmaso et al., 2020). Proposed explanations for this decline include a general slowing of processing speed (Salthouse, 1996) and age-related changes in the neural networks supporting social attention (Kuhn et al., 2015; Slessor et al., 2008). In the present study, a young adult sample was tested, so that the effects of agency would not be confounded with age-related changes in attention.

Anxiety, already discussed in relation to fearful faces, has also been investigated as a stable trait modulating social attention. As we have seen, anxious individuals show an enhanced gaze-cueing effect for fearful faces, suggesting that they are hypervigilant to social signals that may indicate threat, and that this hypervigilance extends to the automatic orienting of attention in response to gaze. First reported in high trait-anxious individuals (Mathews et al., 2003), this finding has since been replicated in trait-anxious individuals (Fox et al., 2007) and extended to state anxiety (Holmes et al., 2006) and to trait fearfulness (Tipples, 2006). However, not all studies have confirmed this effect. Talipski et al. (2021), in a large sample of female participants, found that social anxiety did not significantly modulate the gaze-cueing effect, despite the effect itself being large and reliable. This inconsistency suggests that the relationship between anxiety and the gaze-cueing effect is complex and may depend on the specific type of anxiety measured, the sample characteristics, or the experimental task. The contrast between these studies is instructive: it demonstrates that not all internal traits modulate the gaze-cueing effect equally, and that the question of which internal factors do and do not influence the effect remains an open empirical issue.

Individual differences in autistic-like traits have also been linked to the magnitude of the gaze-cueing effect. Scores on the Autism-Spectrum Quotient correlate negatively with the size of the effect (Bayliss et al., 2005; Bayliss & Tipper, 2005), and individuals with high autistic-like traits show reduced modulation of the gaze-cueing effect by social factors, such as the trustworthiness of the cueing face (Hudson et al., 2012). In autism, however, atypical gaze processing is not simply a matter of reduced orienting: individuals may look at a person’s face when required, yet fail to follow that person’s gaze to the correct target, suggesting difficulties in interpreting the social cue rather than only in allocating attention (Riby et al., 2013). Individuals with Williams syndrome, by contrast, tend to over-attend to faces, yet also struggle to interpret gaze cues accurately (Riby et al., 2013), indicating that both under- and over-attending to social information can disrupt the typical development of social cognition. Together, these findings show that the gaze-cueing effect varies systematically with individual differences in social-cognitive processing, which suggests that its modulation by a higher-order state such as the sense of agency may also vary across individuals. The present study tested a neurotypical sample, allowing an initial characterization of this modulation while leaving open the possibility of future research with clinical populations.

Personality traits have also been linked to individual differences in the gaze-cueing effect. Ponari et al. (2013) found different gaze-cueing effect patterns in extraverts and introverts. Similarly, individuals with low trait self-esteem show a larger gaze-cueing effect, likely reflecting a stronger need to reconnect with others (Wilkowski et al., 2009). Even political temperament appears to play a role: the gaze-cueing effect is reduced, or even absent, in self-defined conservatives compared to liberals, possibly because conservatives tend to be more individualistic and less inclined to be influenced by others (Dodd et al., 2011; Carraro et al., 2015). Importantly, this modulation did not emerge with arrow cues (Carraro et al., 2015), indicating that such traits specifically affect orienting to social cues.


Beyond stable traits, the observer’s temporary internal states can also modulate the gaze-cueing effect, and these findings are the most closely related to the present study. Wilkowski et al. (2009) asked participants to write about past experiences of social acceptance or rejection before the gaze-cueing task, and found a larger gaze-cueing effect after rejection-related thoughts. Using a different manipulation, Capellini et al. (2019) induced social exclusion through the Cyberball task and found the opposite pattern: excluded participants showed a reduced gaze-cueing effect, while the same manipulation had no effect on orienting to arrows, confirming the social nature of the modulation. After social exclusion, individuals appear to withdraw from processing social signals, a pattern that has been interpreted as reflecting a temporary reduction in the motivation to engage with the social environment (Capellini et al., 2019). Although the two studies disagree on the direction of the effect, possibly because of differences in the strength of the induced experience (Dalmaso et al., 2020), both show that affiliative states can shape gaze cueing. Finally, Cui et al. (2014) primed participants by asking them to recall or imagine situations in which they had controlled others (high power) or had been controlled by others (low power), and found a larger gaze-cueing effect after low-power priming, particularly in female participants.

Together, these studies demonstrate that the gaze-cueing effect is sensitive not only to the properties of the face, to the relationship with it, or to stable traits, but also to the observer’s current internal state. The findings of Cui et al. (2014) are especially relevant, as they suggest that a feeling of having or lacking control can shape how strongly attention follows another person’s gaze. They thus provide a direct precedent for the idea that another internal state closely related to control, the sense of agency, could modulate the gaze-cueing effect, a possibility that, to our knowledge, has not yet been investigated and that is the focus of the present work. Unlike social power, however, which was induced before the task as a general feeling of control over other people, the sense of agency in the present experiments arises within each trial, from the participant's own action over the very gaze that cues attention.

The first chapter describes that gaze cueing as a rapid, largely automatic form of social attention that is nonetheless sensitive to who the cueing face is, to the relationship with it, and to the observer's own traits and current states. However, to speculate whether it is plausible that the sense of agency can modulate gaze cueing, we first need to understand what the sense of agency is and how it arises and what possible effects it has on perception and attentional alocation.
