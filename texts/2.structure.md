# Research Questions

### Primary

**RQ1.** Does action–outcome consistency modulate the magnitude and temporal dynamics of the gaze cueing effect?
- *Conceptually:* Does changing the relationship between the participant's action and the avatar's gaze influence how strongly the participant follows the avatar's gaze?

**RQ1a.** Is the gaze cueing effect different across the consistent, inconsistent, and control conditions?
→ *Answered by:* 2 × 3 rANOVA on mean RT per experiment

**RQ1b.** At what stage of processing does the modulation of the gaze cueing effect emerge?
→ *Answered by:* 5-bin analysis (optional; conduct for experiment with strongest effect)

**RQ2.** Does increasing the range of the participant's movement strengthen the effect of action–outcome consistency on the gaze cueing effect?
→ *Compares:* Experiment 1 (button press) vs. Experiment 2 (mouse movement) vs. Experiment 3 (pointing/touch)
→ *Answered by:* Mixed ANOVA — Experiment (3, between-subjects) × Gaze congruency (2, within) × Action consistency (3, within); key term: Experiment × Action consistency interaction; additionally compare GCE effect sizes (η²) across experiments

### Secondary

**RQ3.** Does the action–outcome consistency manipulation produce corresponding differences in explicit sense of agency?
→ *Answered by:* 3 × 3 rANOVA on SoA Likert ratings (question type × action consistency)
→ *Role:* Validates that AOC is an effective explicit SoA manipulation. The design does not allow a direct causal test of whether SoA *causes* changes in gaze cueing — but if AOC affects both GCE and explicit SoA ratings, the findings are consistent with that interpretation.

### Theoretical Extension *(not a formal research question)*
- How do internally generated action-related predictions interact with socially derived attentional cues?
- What other internal states can influence gaze cueing and social allocation of attention?

### Part I — Social Attention and Gaze Cuing *(6–7 pages/10 p max)*

**Why do humans follow other people's gaze?**
- Social cognition and the need to select where to allocate attention; social vs. symbolic cues
- Joint attention and theory of mind as building blocks of social cognition and communication
- The special status of eye contact; the eye as a uniquely readable social signal
- Evolutionary importance of gaze following; gaze as a signal readable across species and across development

> 📌 Optional: discuss what makes social cues special vs. arrows (symbolic cues) — only if space allows.

**What is the gaze cuing effect?**
- Definition and paradigm: Posner spatial cueing paradigm adapted for social stimuli — a face looks left or right, then a target appears either in the cued direction (congruent) or the opposite (incongruent); participants respond faster on congruent trials; the *RT advantage* = mean RT(incongruent) − mean RT(congruent), measured in milliseconds; this difference is the GCE
- Critically, the gaze is *non-predictive* (50/50 left/right) — participants cannot use it strategically, yet it still speeds responses; this demonstrates that gaze shifts attention automatically, not just because it is useful
- Both dynamic (moving eyes in a video/animation) and static (photograph of averted gaze) stimuli produce the effect; both are valid paradigm variants
- Neural correlates: STS encodes gaze direction; TPJ integrates social attention and agency signals; fronto-parietal networks implement the attentional shift

> 📌 Dynamic vs. static gaze: only introduce both here. Detailed comparison of magnitude and mechanisms goes in Part II — Face factors.

**Why does gaze cueing matter — what does it enable?**
- Joint attention: GCE is the mechanism by which two people come to share attentional focus on the same object or location — without this, coordinated perception and action would require explicit verbal instruction at every step
- Social inference: by following where others look, we gain rapid access to what they find relevant, surprising, or threatening — their gaze is a window into their mental state and intentions
- Communication and language: joint attention is a prerequisite for reference (pointing, labelling) and is foundational for language acquisition in development (Tomasello et al.)
- Coordination and survival: evolutionarily, following conspecifics' gaze detects threats and resources without independent scanning — the GCE is the attentional expression of this capacity
- GCE is the dependent variable of interest in this thesis precisely because it indexes a fundamental, largely automatic social-attentional process — if agency (via AOC) can modulate it, that tells us something important about how internal states interact with the most basic level of social perception

**Is gaze cuing automatic?**
- Large literature with mixed findings
- Classic view: purely reflexive and not modulated by top-down factors
- Current view: top-down modulation is possible (Dalmaso, 2020)
- development of GC
- Conclusion: if gaze cuing is not purely reflexive, internal cognitive states — including those created by one's own actions — may influence it → leads to Part II

### Part III — Sense of Agency *(5 pages)*

**What is sense of agency?**
- Authorship: the feeling that "I did this / I caused this outcome" — distinct from merely observing an event
- Sense of agency (SoA) vs. sense of ownership (SoO): SoO = "this body movement is mine"; SoA = "I caused this external outcome"; dissociable in rubber hand illusion, anarchic hand syndrome, somatoparaphrenia — treat as separate constructs
- General review: Haggard (2017) "Sense of agency in the human brain", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* — most comprehensive single source

---

**Classification I — Implicit vs. explicit SoA** *(most widely used distinction)*
- *Implicit SoA*: measured via temporal binding (intentional binding — perceived compression of the interval between action and outcome); automatic, not consciously accessible; driven by low-level sensorimotor prediction; Haggard, Clark & Kalogeras (2002) *Nature*; review: Moore & Obhi (2012)
- *Explicit SoA*: measured via verbal ratings or forced-choice attribution; reflective, belief-based, accessible to introspection; Synofzik, Vosgerau & Newen (2008) *Consciousness & Cognition*
- **Key empirical finding:** implicit and explicit measures frequently *dissociate* — they are not well correlated and respond differently to experimental manipulations; Moore et al. (2012); Dewey & Knoblich (2014)
- Social and contextual cues weight more heavily on explicit SoA; low-level sensorimotor signals weight more on implicit
- This thesis uses explicit (Likert) ratings: temporal binding requires precise interval estimation impractical in a rapid trial-by-trial colour-discrimination paradigm; Likert ratings per trial-set allow direct condition-level comparison; the social/gaze context is expected to weight more heavily on explicit judgments

---

**Classification II — Feeling of agency vs. judgment of agency** *(Synofzik et al., 2008 — most cited two-level model)*
- *Feeling of agency*: pre-reflective, implicit, nonconceptual; arises during action preparation from sensorimotor prediction; the "low-level" layer
- *Judgment of agency*: reflective, explicit, conceptual, belief-like; arises after the outcome by comparing predicted vs. actual outcome + integrating contextual information; the "high-level" layer
- Maps onto implicit/explicit but emphasises *timing*: feeling precedes the action, judgment follows the outcome
- Elaborated further by Pacherie (2008) and Apps & Tsakiris (2014)

---

**Classification III — Prospective vs. retrospective cues** *(Chambon & Haggard, 2012; Chambon, Moore & Haggard, 2014)*
(maybe)
- *Prospective cues*: action fluency, intention clarity, preparation — arise before or during the action; predict SoA even before the outcome
- *Retrospective cues*: outcome monitoring, action-outcome matching — arise after the outcome; feedback-based
- A robust SoA integrates both; balance is context-dependent
- Relevance for this thesis: initiation time (ANCOVA covariate) captures the prospective/temporal-prediction component; the AOC condition effect captures the retrospective/matching component

---

**Classification IV — Low-level vs. high-level agency**
(maybe)
- *Low-level*: sensorimotor prediction, fast, automatic, implicit — the comparator model operates here
- *High-level*: belief-based causal inference, slow, explicit, context-sensitive — Wegner's principles operate here
- Social cues (like gaze direction) weight more at the high/explicit level — supports use of Likert ratings in a social-gaze paradigm; Desantis, Roussel & Waszak (2011); Beyer, Sidarus et al. (2017)

---

**Theoretical models of SoA generation**

| Model | Core claim | Key reference |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| Comparator model | SoA from match between efference copy (predicted sensory consequence) and actual sensory feedback | Frith, Blakemore & Wolpert (2000); Blakemore & Frith (2003) |
| Wegner's apparent mental causation | SoA is postdictive inference: requires priority (thought precedes action), exclusivity (no other cause), consistency (thought fits outcome) | Wegner (2002) *The Illusion of Conscious Will* |
| Cue integration / Bayesian | SoA = weighted integration of multiple prospective + retrospective cues, weighted by reliability; unifies comparator and Wegner | Moore & Haggard (2010); Synofzik et al. (2008); Wolpe et al. (2014) |
| Predictive processing | SoA from precision-weighted prediction errors across hierarchical levels | Apps & Tsakiris (2014); Friston et al. |

Key conclusion across all models: **agency is strongest when action → expected outcome → observed outcome match.** This is precisely what the AOC manipulation tests.

---

**Components of SoA — and how they map onto this design**

SoA is not a single thing. Decomposing it into four components shows what each part of the analysis actually measures:

| Component | What it is | Role in this design |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Sense of Ownership (SoO)** | "my body is moving" | Present in ALL conditions incl. control (participants always make a response action); magnitude varies *across experiments* (keypress < mouse < pointing); cannot explain condition differences *within* an experiment |
| **Temporal prediction** | "I know *when* the target will arrive" | Present in action conditions (you act → wait → target); removed by ANCOVA (covariate = initiation time); captures the prospective cue / intentional binding component |
| **Intentionality / volition** | "I meant to cause *this specific direction*" | Varies across AOC conditions: consistent = fulfilled, inconsistent = violated, control = absent; this is what AOC operationalises; captures the retrospective matching component |
| **Full SoA** | All components together | Captured by the condition effect in ANOVA |

**Why does action–outcome consistency matter?**
- Prediction and prediction error: a mismatch signals the observed outcome was not self-caused (Wegner's consistency criterion; comparator mismatch signal)
- Control and volition: the feeling of having chosen and influenced the outcome (vs. it occurring randomly or externally) is central to SoA; AOC directly manipulates whether that feeling is confirmed or violated
- Action-effect binding (ideomotor theory, Hommel et al., 2001; Kühn et al., 2011): actions are mentally represented in terms of their anticipated sensory effects *before* the outcome occurs; when you press a key/move a mouse/point, your cognitive system pre-activates the expected consequence (e.g., gaze goes left); when the observed outcome matches that pre-activated effect, SoA is confirmed; when it doesn't (inconsistent), SoA is disrupted
- AOC is the manipulation; intentionality is the construct it instantiates — they are operationally the same in this paradigm
- Pathological cases as boundary validators: reduced SoA in schizophrenia (thought insertion, delusions of control); inflated in OCD — confirm SoA is a real construct with identifiable neural signatures
- Conclusion: action-outcome consistency changes internal cognitive processing → leads to Part IV

**Bridge — the gap in non-social research:**
Almost all of the above studies involve *objects* or *non-social sensory events*. Whether AOC-generated agency changes attention to a *social cue that the observer caused* is an open question. To address it, we first need to ask whether people can experience agency over social outcomes at all.

**Can our actions change how we perceive other people?**

- Self-other coupling: acting together changes how we predict and process each other's behaviour
- Agency in social interaction as an extension of basic SoA — the outcome is another agent's state, not a physical event

**Can people experience agency over another person's behaviour?**(maybe)
- Evidence pathway: (1) SoA over mechanical/physical outcomes is well established; (2) extending to biological/social outcomes is less obvious but has been demonstrated in controlled paradigms
- Gaze-contingent paradigms: paradigms in which an avatar's or confederate's gaze responds contingently to the participant's action — participants rate their sense of control as higher when the gaze is contingent on their action than when it is not
- Virtual agents and avatars: participants feel agency over avatar behaviour when their actions predictably determine it; the contingency (= AOC) is the key variable, not the biological nature of the stimulus
- Conclusion: when an action reliably and consistently determines another person's (or avatar's) gaze direction, people can and do experience agency over that gaze

### Part V — Rationale for the Current Study *(3 pages)*

> 📌 This section has two jobs: (1) explain how the gap is addressed — paradigm + operationalisation; (2) state formal hypotheses. It is the bridge between the literature and the empirical chapters.

**The gap, restated concisely**
- The literature establishes: GCE is flexible; SoA changes perception and attention; people can feel agency over social gaze
- What is missing: no study has asked whether AOC over a gaze cue changes how strongly that cue orients attention
- "To the best of our knowledge, no previous research has directly tested whether causing another person's gaze direction — and the degree to which that action matches its outcome — modulates the magnitude of the gaze cueing effect."
- The present study addresses this gap directly


**AOC as operationalisation of agency**
- AOC (action-outcome consistency) is the operational manipulation: it directly determines whether intentionality is fulfilled (consistent), violated (inconsistent), or absent (control)
- Explicit SoA ratings (Likert) serve as converging evidence that the AOC manipulation produces the intended psychological state — they validate that participants experience the conditions as differing in agency
- The RT data and Likert ratings together allow the argument: if AOC changes attention *and* AOC changes reported agency, the findings are consistent with the claim that agency modulates gaze cueing

> 📌 Note: the design does not allow a direct causal test of whether SoA is the mediating mechanism — that would require mediation analysis with trial-level SoA measures, which is not possible here.

**Hypotheses**
to be stated in the rationale (not call them hypothesis - expectations is better)
*For each experiment (RQ1 and RQ3):*

**H1:** The gaze cueing effect (RT advantage for congruent over incongruent trials) will be largest in the consistent condition, intermediate in the control condition, and smallest (or absent/reversed) in the inconsistent condition.

**H2:** Explicit SoA ratings will be highest in the consistent condition and lowest in the inconsistent condition, with control ratings intermediate — confirming that AOC is an effective SoA manipulation.

*Across experiments (RQ2):*

**H3:** The modulation of the GCE by AOC will increase across experiments as the range and directness of the participant's movement increases (keypress < mouse < pointing), reflecting either stronger intentionality signals or greater SoO from increased motor involvement.

**Paradigm overview**
- Participants view an avatar face whose gaze direction they can cause through their own action
- Action conditions: participant performs an action (keypress / mouse movement / pointing) → avatar looks left or right depending on their action
- Consistent condition: avatar looks in the direction the participant intended (action-outcome match → high SoA)
- Inconsistent condition: avatar looks in the opposite direction (action-outcome mismatch → low SoA)
- Control condition: no participant action; avatar gaze direction is predetermined → baseline GCE with no SoA
- After each set of trials: three Likert questions assessing explicit SoA; the specific question words — *predict*, *caused*, and *control* — were chosen to target distinct SoA dimensions established in Part III: *predict* targets the prospective cue component (Classification III — did you anticipate the direction before the gaze moved?); *caused* targets retrospective causal attribution (Classification IV — did you bring this specific outcome about?); *control* targets volition and sustained intentional control (Classification IV — did you determine the outcome?); *caused* and *control* are both Classification IV but tap different aspects: causal attribution is momentary and event-specific, whereas control implies sustained directional agency; justify this word choice here in the paradigm description, not in the Discussion — it is a theoretical design decision that the reader needs to understand before encountering the data
- Three experiments: same paradigm, increasing embodiment of the causing action — keypress (Exp 1) → mouse movement (Exp 2) → touchscreen pointing (Exp 3)

**3.4 Does AOC produce differences in explicit SoA? (RQ3)**
- Rating patterns across conditions and experiments: consistent > control > inconsistent expected for all question types
- Do RT effects and SoA ratings co-vary? Implications for the AOC-as-SoA-manipulation interpretation
- Caveat: explicit ratings are a separate block; correlation with RT data is possible but interpret with caution
- **Question type × action consistency interaction *(if significant)*:** different aspects of explicit SoA may respond differently to AOC:
  - *Predict* ratings: prospective (Classification III); should track action conditions vs. control more than consistent vs. inconsistent — control removes the action entirely, so there is nothing to predict
  - *Caused* ratings: retrospective causal attribution (Classification IV); should differentiate action conditions from control most strongly — control involves no participant action so causal attribution is absent; within action conditions, may also differentiate consistent from inconsistent (gaze going against the intended direction reduces felt causation)
  - *Control* ratings: volition and sustained intentional control (Classification IV); may be most sensitive to the consistent vs. inconsistent distinction — controlling implies the outcome followed your intention, which is violated in the inconsistent condition
  - If only *caused* and *control* differ (not *predict*): AOC affects retrospective causal and volitional aspects but not temporal prediction
  - If all three differentiate equally: AOC affects all dimensions of explicit SoA uniformly → cleaner manipulation

**3.5 Theoretical implications**
- For GCE models: does self-causation (via AOC) modulate the reflexive component of social attention?
- For SoA models: social gaze direction as a special class of action outcome
- For predictive processing: how internally generated action predictions interact with socially derived attentional cues
- **If GCE reversed in inconsistent condition *(if observed)*:** internal action representation overrides the external social cue; in agentic contexts, prediction takes priority over observed social signals; implications for GCE automaticity debate
- Fallback interpretation: if SoA manipulation fails (no condition differences in Likert ratings) — reframe as SoO (body movement) vs. social cueing, within attentional control literature; the across-experiment pattern would then be driven by movement extent rather than intentionality

**3.6 Limitations**
- No eye-tracking to confirm cursor-following in Experiment 2 (inference supported by literature)
- SoA measured only via explicit Likert ratings; no implicit measure (temporal binding)
- Separate question block limits direct trial-level AOC–SoA correlation
- Sample sizes; ecological validity; constrained SOA range

**3.7 Future directions**
- Eye-tracking in Experiment 2 to directly test the eye-hand coupling hypothesis
- Experiment manipulating Action→Target and Gaze→Target independently to test competing predictions
- Implicit SoA measure (temporal binding) alongside explicit ratings
- Bidirectional question: does processing another person's gaze also shape the experience of agency?
- Individual differences: agency sensitivity, social anxiety, autism spectrum
- Clinical populations; HRI and virtual agent applications

## Why This Structure Works

The argument builds in one direction — each part of the literature review answers one question and creates a gap that the next part fills:

> **Humans follow other people's gaze**
> ↓
> **Gaze cueing is flexible and context-dependent, not purely automatic**
> ↓
> **No prior work manipulates the observer's own immediately preceding action**
> ↓
> **Actions create a sense of agency through action–outcome consistency**
> ↓
> **Agency changes perception and attention — but only for non-social stimuli so far**
> ↓
> **People can experience agency over social outcomes, including another's gaze**
> ↓
> **Therefore: AOC-driven agency may change social attentional orienting — nobody has tested this**

The three experiments then test this across an embodiment gradient (keypress → mouse+eyes → touchscreen pointing), which adds a second dimension: not just *whether* AOC modulates the GCE, but *whether the directness and embodiment of the causing action matters* (RQ2).
