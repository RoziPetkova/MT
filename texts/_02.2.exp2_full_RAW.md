# Experiment 2

## Method
Experiment 2 employed the same as experiment 1, described in the capter.., part.. Rationale. The experiment differed only in the response modality of the direction-selection response: instead of pressing a keyboard button, participants indicated their choice with a mouse click.

As in the prevous experiment, a fixation symbol preceded each trial: a cross ("+") or an asterisk ("\*"), one signaling a control trial and the other an action trial requiring a direction-selection response; In experiment 2 together with the onset of the action-trial symbol, the mouse cursor became visible at the center of the screen, just below the fixation symbol; it remained hidden throughout control trials. Afterwords just like in Experiment 1 the face then appeared together with two bordered, unfilled rectangles positioned to its left and right — the same locations later occupied by the color target. Participants selected the gaze direction they wished to induce by moving the cursor trough the mouse into one of the two rectangles and clicking; the direction was scored according to which rectangle contained the cursor at the moment of the click. Clicks falling outside both rectangles were not registered and had no effect, such that only a click inside one of the two rectangles ended the response window. (should it be added ot not about the gaze following the cursor?)
Unlike Experiment 1, due to the nature of the action for the direction selection task, there was no counterbalancing of hand usadge between task. 

Explicit sense of agency was again assessed at the end of the procedure using the same three self-report questions (predict / control / caused).

### Participants

Sixty-one students took part in the experiment. One participant was excluded due to a technical problem during data collection and is not present in the dataset, leaving data from sixty participants (11 males, 49 females; M = 22.78 years, SD = 7.16). Participats were given ECTS credits tied to accuracy performance as in the previous experiment, and all participants provided written informed consent and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision.

## Results

**Data Exclusion and Final Sample**

Data were initially collected from 61 participants. One participant was excluded due to a technical problem during data collection. Four participants were excluded because their accuracy in the main experimental phase was below 85%, resulting in a sample of 56 participants. Seven further participants were excluded based on predefined questionnaire data-quality criteria — invariant responding across items, defined as a dominant response exceeding 75% of all ratings combined with a condition-level response range of 0.5 or less (Meade & Craig, 2012); This resulted in a sample of 49 participants, contributing 8,232 trials.

From these trials, 3.88% (319 trials) were removed as error trials, leaving 7,913 trials. Fourty-three trials (0.54%, spread across 14 participants) were with reaction time lower than 200 ms, therefor were also excluded. A further 4.40% (346 trials) were excluded because their remaining reaction times deviated by more than 2 standard deviations from the mean, calculated separately for each participant and condition, leaving 7,524 trials.

One further participant was excluded due to their overall mean reaction time exceeded the group one by more than 2 standard deviations (cutoff: 708.7 ms, participant's mean: 715.3 ms).

The final analyses were therefore conducted on data from 48 participants.

**Statistical analyses**

A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G\*Power 3.1.9.6 (Faul et al., 2007). It indicated that with the final sample size of 48 participants, the experiment had high power (power = 0.972) to detect even a small effect (f = 0.20). For within-subject terms with more than one degree of freedom, the Greenhouse and Geisser (1959) correction was applied to control for violations of sphericity and the associated inflation of Type I error; simple effects were subsequently examined using Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons at p \< .05.

**rANOVA**

As in Experiment 1, reaction times were averaged per participant and submitted to a repeated-measures ANOVA, with gaze congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) and action consistency (consistent, inconsistent, control) entered as within-subject factors.

Replicating Experiment 1, there was a significant main effect of gaze congruency, *F*(1, 47) = 17.37, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .270, indicating that RTs reliably differed between congruent and incongruent trials; as the descriptive statistics above show, this difference reflected faster responses on congruent than on incongruent trials, indicating the overall existance of standard Gaze-cueing effect. The simple main effect of gaze, however, showed that the congruency (gaze-cueing) effect was significant within the consistent (*F*(1, 47) = 34.38, *p* \< .001) and control conditions (*F*(1, 47) = 7.10, *p* = .011), but not within the inconsistent condition (*F*(1, 47) = 3.43, *p* = .070).

The main effect of action consistency was also significant, *F*(1.29, 60.56) = 13.28, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .220 (greenhouse-Geisser corrected). 
Post hoc comparisons (collapsed across gaze congruency) showed that both active conditions - consistent (M = 509.36 ms; *t*(47) = −3.38, *p* = .004, Cohen's *d* = −0.21) and inconsistent trials (M = 505.21 ms; *t*(47) = −4.16, *p* \< .001, *d* = −0.26) were faster than control trials (M = 529.80 ms), while consistent and inconsistent did not differ from each other (*p* = .349) — participants responded faster to the target appearance whenever an action was required in advance (direction-selection task), though the magnitude of this effect was small (|d| ≈ 0.2–0.26).

More importantly, the Gaze Congruency × Action Consistency interaction was significant, *F*(1.90, 89.38) = 12.17, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .206 — unlike Experiment 1, where the equivalent interaction did not reach significance. To investigate the interaction, post hoc conditional comparisons were conducted for each factor (gaze congruency and action consistency). 

Post hoc comparisons conditioned on gaze showed a significant difference between the active action conditions (consistent and inconsistent) at both levels of gaze congruency, but with the direction of that difference reversed between them.

In congruent trials (target appeared where the face gazed), participants were fastest when their chosen direction matched the actual gaze direction (consistent condition — e.g., chose left, gaze went left, target appeared left). This consistent condition was significantly faster than both inconsistent (MD = −15.19 ms, SE = 4.78, *t*(47) = −3.18, *p* = .008, *d* = −0.16) and control (MD = −27.96 ms, SE = 7.38, *t*(47) = −3.79, *p* = .001, *d* = −0.29) ; inconsistent and control did not differ significantly from each other (MD = −12.77 ms, *t*(47) = −1.89, *p* = .195). In incongruent trials, the pattern flipped: the inconsistent condition — where the chosen direction did not match the gaze (e.g., chose left, gaze went right, target appeared left) — was now fastest, significantly faster than both consistent (MD = 23.49 ms, SE = 5.25, *t*(47) = 4.48, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.25) and control (MD = −36.40 ms, SE = 7.50, *t*(47) = −4.85, *p* \< .001, *d* = −0.38); consistent and control again did not differ significantly (MD = −12.91 ms, *t*(47) = −1.96, *p* = .168). This suggests that in incongruent trials, the standard gaze-cueing effect did not reliably hold — consistent with the simple main effect of gaze reported above, which showed no significant gaze-cueing effect in the inconsistent condition (*p* = .070). 

A post hoc conditioned on action showed faster responces for congruent trials within the consistent condition (27.92 ms, *SE* = 4.76, *t*(47) = 5.86, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.30) and within the control condition (12.87 ms, *SE* = 4.83, *t*(47) = 2.66, *p* = .011, *d* = 0.13), but in the inconsistent condition the effect was numerically reversed and did not reach significance (−10.76 ms, *SE* = 5.81, *t*(47) = 1.85, *p* = .070, *d* = 0.12). 

**ANCOVA Results**

As in Experiment 1, a repeated-measures ANCOVA was conducted with mean initiation time included as a covariate. Controlling for initiation time did not change the overall pattern of results: the main effects of Gaze, *F*(1, 46) = 17.95, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .281, and Action, *F*(1.36, 62.64) = 16.29, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .262, and the Gaze × Action interaction, *F*(1.90, 87.46) = 11.92, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .206, all remained significant and closely matched their plain-rANOVA counterparts above. The Action × covariate interaction also reached significance (*F*(1.36, 62.64) = 11.65, *p* \< .001), likely because initiation time means something different in the no-action control condition; it should not be interpreted theoretically.

**Correlation**

Pearson correlations among the six RT cells and mean initiation time were computed. Initiation time correlated strongly with RT in every condition (*r* = .50–.67, all *p* \< .001) — unlike Experiment 1, where no such relationship held in the active conditions, consistent with the greater motor preparation a reach-and-click response requires relative to a simple keypress.


This indicates that the magnitude of the gaze-cueing effect genuinely differed across action-consistency conditions in Experiment 2.
In other words, the standard gaze-cueing advantage for congruent trials held reliably when participants either made a matching choice or made no choice at all, but this advantage did not clearly hold — and numerically flipped — when the gaze moved opposite to the participant's chosen direction.


Experiment 2 also replicates the standard gaze-cueing effect and the action main effect (driven purely by "any action vs. no action," not by consistent vs. inconsistent). But critically unlike in Experimet 1, the Gaze × Action interaction was this time significant. Decomposing it: the gaze-cueing effect held reliably when the outcome matched the participant's choice (consistent) or when no choice was made (control), but broke down (and numerically reversed) when the outcome contradicted their choice (inconsistent). In other words a mismatch between one's chosen action and its outcome specifically disrupts the reflexive gaze-cueing effect, a modulation that only emerges with the richer motor/decision act. This pattern survived controlling for general processing speed (initiation time) via ANCOVA.


**Questionary data**

**Descriptives**

As in Experiment 1, the three questions were answered on a seven-point Likert scale. Descriptive statistics for all questionnaire items across the three action-consistency conditions are presented in Table X.

Ratings followed the same ordering seen in Experiment 1 — consistent > inconsistent > control — but were compressed into a narrower and lower range throughout. In the consistent condition, mean ratings were highest for causation (M = 3.88, SD = 1.59), followed by prediction (M = 3.69, SD = 1.14) and control (M = 3.32, SD = 1.35). In the inconsistent condition, means were 3.76 (SD = 1.53) for causation, 3.40 (SD = 1.04) for prediction, and 3.07 (SD = 1.27) for control. Unlike Experiment 1, the control (passive) condition did not sit near the floor of the scale: mean ratings here ranged from 2.18 (SD = 1.07) for causation to 2.98 (SD = 1.25) for prediction.

This indicates that participants still distinguished action-generated from externally generated gaze movements, though less starkly than with a keypress response. Regarding question type, causation was again rated highest in both active conditions, though the margin over the other two items was smaller than in Experiment 1. In the control condition, prediction — not causation — received the highest ratings, replicating the reversal already observed in Experiment 1.

**Analyses**

As in Experiment 1, ratings were submitted to a 3 (Question type: caused, controlled, predicted) × 3 (Action Consistency: consistent, inconsistent, control) repeated-measures ANOVA, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied to all within-subject terms with more than one degree of freedom. Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated departures from normality in three of the nine cells, concentrated in the control condition; as before, the analysis is reported as planned given the aggregated dependent variable and the size of the effects below.

The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Action Consistency, *F*(1.73, 81.10) = 30.57, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .394: participants again reliably distinguished between the three conditions in how they answered the sense-of-agency questions, replicating the manipulation check from Experiment 1, though with a substantially smaller effect size than the η²ₚ = .673 found there. A significant main effect of Question type, *F*(1.64, 77.01) = 3.52, *p* = .043, η²ₚ = .070, showed that the three questions again differed in overall level of endorsement, though far more weakly than in Experiment 1 (η²ₚ = .343). These two effects were qualified by a significant Action Consistency × Question type interaction, *F*(2.18, 102.31) = 11.14, *p* \< .001, η²ₚ = .192 — again smaller than in Experiment 1 (η²ₚ = .403) — indicating that the discrimination between conditions was not equally strong across all three questions. As in Experiment 1, both directions of this interaction were examined in turn, each as its own Holm-corrected family of simple effects.

**Does action consistency affect the three questions differently?**

Simple main effects of Action Consistency were examined within each question type. Consistency significantly modulated ratings for all three questions — causation, *F*(2, 94) = 33.06, *p* \< .001; control, *F*(2, 94) = 23.40, *p* \< .001; prediction, *F*(2, 94) = 8.32, *p* \< .001 — replicating Experiment 1's finding that all three items are sensitive to the manipulation, again with causation showing the strongest sensitivity and prediction the weakest.

Holm-corrected pairwise comparisons revealed a pattern that departed from Experiment 1 in one respect: for all three questions, consistent and inconsistent trials no longer differed significantly from each other (causation, *p* = .309, *d* = 0.09; control, *p* = .083, *d* = 0.19; prediction, *p* = .099, *d* = 0.22), whereas in Experiment 1 this comparison had reached significance for causation and control. What remained intact was the separation between the active conditions and control: for causation, both consistent (*p* \< .001, *d* = 1.31) and inconsistent (*p* \< .001, *d* = 1.22) exceeded control; the control item showed the same pattern (consistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.78; inconsistent vs. control, *p* \< .001, *d* = 0.59); and for prediction, both active conditions again exceeded control (consistent vs. control, *p* = .001, *d* = 0.55; inconsistent vs. control, *p* = .026, *d* = 0.32). In other words, participants in Experiment 2 registered whether they had made a choice at all, but not reliably whether that choice matched the outcome.

**Do the three questions differ from each other within the same condition?**

The reverse simple main effects — Question type within each level of Action Consistency — showed a different pattern from Experiment 1. Question type no longer significantly affected ratings in the consistent condition, *F*(2, 94) = 2.95, *p* = .057, and only marginally did so in the inconsistent condition, *F*(2, 94) = 4.23, *p* = .017; both are weaker than the strongly significant effects found for these same conditions in Experiment 1 (both *p* \< .001). In the control condition, question type remained clearly significant, *F*(2, 94) = 13.17, *p* \< .001, matching Experiment 1.

Because the simple effects in the consistent and inconsistent conditions did not reach significance, pairwise comparisons within those two conditions were not examined further. In the control condition, the same reversal seen in Experiment 1 replicated: prediction was rated higher than both causation (*p* \< .001, *d* = −0.62) and control (*p* = .003, *d* = −0.52), while causation and control did not differ from each other (*p* = .444, *d* = −0.09).

Overall, the questionnaire results in Experiment 2 replicate the two central findings from Experiment 1 — the action-consistency manipulation still shifts explicit sense-of-agency ratings, and the three questions are not interchangeable — but both effects are considerably attenuated, and the finer-grained distinction between consistent and inconsistent trials, present in Experiment 1, is no longer detectable on any of the three questions.
