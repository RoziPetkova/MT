# Introduction

"Hell is other people," wrote Sartre (1944), suggesting that others are what block and anguish us. Yet humans are highly social and communicative creatures. We live in a social environment and, therefore, organize and structure our functioning according to the rules and phenomena that constitute it. To orient ourselves in that environment, we depend on information that comes, to a large extent, from other people. We constantly read the signals others send us: verbal cues such as language, but also non-verbal ones such as posture, head orientation, pointing gestures and gaze (Tomasello et al., 2005). Among these non-verbal cues, gaze holds a privileged status, because it signals not only where another person is looking, but also what they are attending to and, potentially, what they intend to do next (Emery, 2000). Following another person's gaze allows us to attend to the same object as they do, the basis of joint attention, and from there to infer what they know, want and intend (Baron-Cohen, 1995; Tomasello et al., 2005).

At the level of basic attentional processing, this tendency is captured by gaze cueing: the shift of one's own attention in the direction of another person's gaze (Driver et al., 1999; Friesen & Kingstone, 1998; Langton & Bruce, 1999). Its behavioural signature, faster responses to whatever appears where another person is looking, is known as the gaze-cueing effect (GCE). Because the effect emerges even when gaze is uninformative, gaze cueing was initially considered a highly automatic, reflexive form of attentional orienting (Friesen & Kingstone, 1998; Frischen et al., 2007). However, a large body of research has since shown that the magnitude of the GCE can be influenced by many factors: the characteristics of the cueing face, such as its emotional expression or social status; the relationship between the face and the observer, such as familiarity or group membership; and the characteristics of the observer, from relatively stable traits such as gender, age, autistic-like or anxiety traits, to more unstable, temporary internal states such as feeling socially excluded or powerless (for reviews, see Dalmaso et al., 2020; McKay et al., 2021; Frischen et al., 2007). Gaze cueing, therefore, is fast, but it is not insensitive to what the observer is currently thinking or feeling.

Another phenomenon that has attracted growing research interest is the sense of agency (SoA): the experience of being the one who initiates and controls one's own actions and, through them, events in the outside world (Haggard, 2017; Haggard & Tsakiris, 2009). How exactly this experience arises is still debated (Haggard, 2017). Some accounts propose that agency results from a match between the predicted and the actual consequences of an action (Blakemore et al., 2000; Frith et al., 2000), others that it is inferred after the fact from contextual cues (Wegner, 2002; Wegner & Wheatley, 1999), and others that the brain combines several such cues, weighting each according to its reliability (Moore et al., 2009; Synofzik et al., 2008). What is well established, however, is that agency does not simply accompany action, but shapes perception and the allocation of attention (Wen & Imamizu, 2022). Events caused by one's own voluntary action are perceived as occurring closer in time to that action (intentional binding; Haggard et al., 2002), and self-produced sensations are perceived as less intense than identical sensations produced by someone else (sensory attenuation; Blakemore et al., 1999). Attention is drawn to objects and locations over which one has control (Wen & Imamizu, 2022), and even planning an action biases attention toward the stimulus features relevant for that action (Fagioli et al., 2007). Agency can also extend to social outcomes: leading another person's gaze, that is, making a face look where one has just looked, produces intentional binding, an implicit marker of agency (Stephenson et al., 2018). Research at the intersection of the two phenomena, however, has so far asked only how gaze affects the sense of agency (Stephenson et al., 2018; Ulloa et al., 2019), and not whether agency over another's gaze changes how strongly that gaze orients one's attention.

With this in mind, the purpose of the present work is to examine whether these two phenomena interact. More specifically, we ask whether intentional actions, which are assumed to give rise to a sense of agency, can influence the gaze-cueing effect. Although gaze cueing has been shown to depend on many contextual and social factors, it remains unknown whether an observer's own actions, movements, and expectations regarding another person's gaze influence attentional orienting. If such an influence exists, it would suggest that the processes that give rise to the sense of agency and those that orient attention in response to another's gaze are not independent, but rely at least in part on shared representations, as has been proposed for action planning and perception more generally (Fagioli et al., 2007; Hommel et al., 2001). It would also show that gaze cueing is sensitive not only to who the cueing face is and who the observer is, but also to what the observer has just done and expected to happen.

To answer these questions, three experiments were conducted. All three used the standard gaze-cueing paradigm, with one additional step: before the face shifted its gaze, participants selected the direction in which it would look, so that they themselves triggered the gaze movement. On some trials, the face then looked in the selected direction (action–outcome consistent); on others, it looked in the opposite direction (action–outcome inconsistent); and on a third set of trials, participants made no choice and the direction was determined by the computer (control). The relation between the face's gaze and the target that followed varied as well: the gaze was either congruent with the target location or incongruent with it. Across the series, the range of motion of the action used to select the gaze direction also varied, from a simple keypress (Experiment 1), through a mouse click (Experiment 2), to a more ecologically realistic movement, a tap on a touchscreen (Experiment 3). The central question of this thesis is therefore: **does action–outcome consistency, and the sense of agency it may produce, modulate gaze cueing?**
